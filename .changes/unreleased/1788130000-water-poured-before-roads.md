title: Water is poured onto the landscape before the roads are drawn
---

Lakes used to be one flat sheet at a single height for the whole world, and
they were drawn a whole 224 m tile at a time — so a lake's edge was the tile
grid rather than the shore, with straight sides and right angles, and the
sheet hung in the air over every metre of ground inside that tile that stood
above the water.

Worse, nothing in the pipeline had ever been asked where the water was
before a road was routed through it. Stages were laid across sea basins and
the terrain quietly raised an embankment to carry them: on one seed, an
eleventh of the route ran over ground under the water table, on a causeway
sixty-three metres tall, with the start line itself in the middle of a lake.
Seventeen seeds in forty started in water.

The water is now worked out from the bare country first, by physics. Rain
runs downhill and collects: a priority flood finds the sill each hollow
would spill over, the groundwater says how much of it is really wet, and a
body's surface is the lower of the two. Every lake and tarn gets its own
flat level, and the shoreline is the ground's own contour — cut against the
landscape the game actually draws, so it follows every headland and inlet at
whatever resolution you look at it, and never lies over dry land.

Because the water is there first, the road can be laid knowing about it: a
stage is sited on ground clear of the water, the route is drawn around the
lakes rather than through them, a road's freeboard clears the water actually
beside it, and a river ends in whatever body it reaches instead of walking
past it looking for the sea.
