---
type: Changed
title: Half the drift, a handbrake that stops you, and three cars that want different pedals
---

Every car slides about half as far as it did. At full lock on gravel the
hatch now holds 10°, the four-wheel-drive 13.5° and the saloon 18°, where it
used to be 19° / 25° / 35°.

The spread between the three is deliberately tighter than it was, because
the thing that actually separates real layouts is not how far sideways they
can be got — it is what KEEPS them there:

- The saloon has a real drift on the throttle. Hold the power and the angle
  stays; catch it on opposite lock.
- The hatch has none at all. Its big angles come from the brake, a flick or
  the lever, and the throttle pulls it straight again — which is now the
  fastest way out of one.
- The coupe is between the two: point it, and it slides a little on the
  power and gathers itself up.

Asked to hold twenty degrees, the saloon settles at 15.8° on the power
against 12.1° off it, and the hatch at 9.9° on against 10.4° OFF. A move
also lifts each car toward a ceiling of its OWN rather than toward the
saloon's, so a provoked hatch is no longer as sideways as a rear-driver.

The car picker is ordered easiest first — hatch, coupe, saloon — and each
car's line now tells you how to DRIVE it rather than what it is.

Corners cost more than they did, which is the same change seen from the
other side: the old model let a 35° drift buy half again the grip the tyres
were supposed to have, so a car that slides less holds a wider line and has
to be braked for. Stage times are about a sixth longer.

The handbrake is a brake. Locking the rear wheels now costs a third of the
car's braking — the rear axle's real share — and a car sideways on the lever
scrubs its speed off far harder, so a full-lock yank from 60 km/h has it
slowed right down inside a second and a half. Its rotation also fades as the
car reaches the angle it was asked for, instead of walking every car in the
roster round to a spin. It is the tool it is in the real thing: tapped, not
held, on the corners nothing else will get round — and the way a
front-driver is snapped into a hairpin.

The rival crews were taught the new car. They plan corners off what the
tyres will actually hand over at the speed they are going rather than off a
flat ceiling, and they spend the drift they really have on a hot entry
rather than the drift they used to have — without which the quick crews
spent the change running wide off the outside of fast sweepers.
