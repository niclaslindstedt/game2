type: Fixed
title: Junctions and embankments that dropped the car off the road
---

A junction between the gravel and the tarmac could be a death trap: the two
roads met at different heights, and the ground beside them fell away as a
sheer wall rather than a slope. Driving off it was a fall you did not come
back from.

Three things were behind it. A branch leaving a junction followed the
country for its height while still running beside the road it had just
left, so the two parted company vertically long before they parted on the
map — seven metres apart in height, three metres apart on the ground. The
terrain then had no good answer: hold the ground up under the branch and it
is a wall on the stage's shoulder; let it go and the branch hangs in the
air.

The road was also allowed to stand any distance off the land it crossed.
Where the ground falls away faster than a road may descend, that came out
as a thirty-metre embankment with a sixty-degree side. And the bench the
terrain draws beside a road — a cutting on one side, fill on the other —
ran on for a hundred metres whether or not it had already met the real
ground, inventing twenty metres of hillside that was not there.

Roads now stay within reach of the country they cross, branches stay inside
the stage's own verge until the two have genuinely separated, and a bench
stops where it meets the ground. Walls beside the road are down by more
than half.

Jumps are fixed too, and for a related reason: a quarter of them were ramps
shallower than the grade a road climbs anyway, which on a descending road
launched nothing at all. A jump is now drawn by the grade of its ramp, so
every one of them throws the car.
