title: Small towns where the tarmac leads
---

The tarmac goes somewhere now. Where a stage borrows enough of a public road
to hold one, a small town of ten to twenty buildings stands along it on both
sides — the rally runs straight through between the walls — and where the
stage only meets a road at a junction or a crossing, the town stands down the
arm the tape shuts, so the road the rally does not take can be seen leading
to it.

A town is a village, not a row of farms: mostly houses in the homestead's own
paints and roofs, with the village's own buildings among them — a three-storey
block of flats with balconies chequered down its front, a grocery with glass
the whole width of its front and a sign over it, the post office in postal
yellow with the postbox by the step, and a workshop with the roller doors in
its gable — and the shops stand in the middle of the street, where a village
keeps them. Cars are parked outside: nosed in across a shop's forecourt,
along the street outside a house.

Every town is placed by the generator, so the same seed always has the same
village, every lot is graded level with the street's own verge, the forest and
the crowd keep off it, and the walls and the parked cars are as solid as they
look. A car that runs wide through a village finds out.
