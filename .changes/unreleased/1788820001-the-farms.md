title: Farms among the homesteads — barns, paddocks with cattle and sheep, fields and machinery
---

Half the taiga's homesteads are farms now. A farm's yard is the bigger one,
and across it from the house stands the barn — a Swedish ladugård, longer,
wider and a storey taller than the house: a stone or rendered byre with its
row of small windows under a hayloft of falu-red boards, a gambrel or a steep
gable roof with ventilators along the ridge, the big sliding door on the
front, the loft door up an earth ramp in one gable, and on half of them a
lean-to machinery shed along the back. Some have a tower silo at the gable.
They vary: red, tarred black or grey, tile or sheet metal, long or longer.

Past the barn or behind the house lies a fenced paddock — a roundpole fence
with a gate in the side nearest the yard — with the stock grazing in it:
red-and-white or black-and-white cattle, or a flock of sheep, wandering,
grazing and looking about on their own. Where the country allows it there is
a field too, ploughed dark in furrows, cut to stubble, or standing hay with
the round bales the baler left across it (a car sends those rolling). The
machinery stands where it was left: the tractor in front of the barn, the
tipping trailer at its gable, the plough, the disc harrow or the baler at the
field's edge.

The barn's walls, the silo, the tractor and the trailer are as solid as a
house and a car; the fence posts are poles a car goes through; the forest
keeps off the grazing and the field; and a ploughed field is soft going.
