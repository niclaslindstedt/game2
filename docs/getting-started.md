# Getting started

## Playing

Open [game2.niclaslindstedt.se](https://game2.niclaslindstedt.se/). The app opens on an attract screen: the studio card while the game loads, then the title under two crossed finish flags and **press any key to start** (tap, on a phone). It waits for you — the press is also what lets the browser turn the sound on. Behind it the game is already running, and clearing the card drops you on the **main menu**, which is not a still: a bot is driving a real stage behind it, seen from a drone, and it keeps going while you decide. Four ways in:

- **Campaign** — a location (the Taiga first, then the Desert behind it) and its six stages, each one unlocked by getting on the **podium** of the one before it, and the location after it unlocked by finishing **top of its table**. The campaign IS its championship: one board, one set of points, nothing stacked on top. Locked stages wear a grey border and a padlock; open ones wear green. The ladder climbs in both length and difficulty, from a 1-minute forest road to seven minutes of hairpins and jumps in a night storm — and then changes discipline: the last two are **circuits**, three laps of a closed lap that comes back to its own start line, where you get to learn the road and the clock is the whole opponent.

  You are not out there alone. Every campaign stage is run against **fourteen other crews**, leaving the start control ten seconds apart the way a real rally does, and you are the last car on the road. The stage opens on the camera circling the start control while the crew in front of you — stood on the line beside you, not on top of you — gets its green and drives away; the ten seconds you are watching are the ten seconds of head start they have. Any pedal cuts it short, and the camera flies quickly to your driving view rather than jumping there. Everybody ahead is genuinely up the road, so there is nothing to overtake for position, only times to beat; but they ARE out there, in their own cars and their own colours, and if you catch one you can touch them. So can they touch you. Your **position** rides in the top right, beside the map, and it moves at every split board: your place is every car already through that board, plus you. The split itself names the crew you are measured against — the leader through it. **Finish in the top three and the stage is cleared**; finish outside it and the card at the line says TOO SLOW, and the stage is still yours to run again. **DIFFICULTY** at the foot of the stage list picks how good the field is — EASY, MEDIUM or HARD, three cards with a meter on them, green to red. It buys every rival crew a bigger budget to spend on their own driving and lets more of their temper off the leash, and it also says what a crash costs YOU: on EASY the car cannot be marked (the tree still stops you dead and still spins you round — it simply leaves nothing behind), MEDIUM keeps half of every hit, and HARD hands you the whole of it. Each card says both halves under its word. Each stage box remembers your best finishing position at the setting you are looking at, beside your best time.

  And every stage is worth **points**: three for the win, two for second, one for third, nothing below that — to you and to the fourteen crews alike. Each stage box carries what it is currently paying you, so the ladder and the table are the same board. At the line the card says what the place paid you and where the location now stands, and the whole sheet is on the card beside it: all fifteen crews in finishing order with a picture of each one's car, their times, what the stage paid each of them, and what they have for the location — eight to a page, opened on the page your own row is on, with arrows to walk the rest. **Run a stage as many times as you like** — for a better place, a better time and better points; the board keeps whichever attempt paid you MORE, so a lap driven for fun can never cost you a title.

  Those points are the whole campaign, at both scales: a stage opens the one after it by paying you something (a podium), and a location opens the next COUNTRY by being driven all the way through with you top of its table — which in practice means winning stages rather than scraping onto podiums. **STANDINGS**, at the top right of the stage list, opens the location's board: where you stand, what you have scored, how much of the country you have driven, and all fifteen crews in order with a picture of every car — the same board the results card shows at the end of a stage, asked what all six of them added up to. **RESET** is on it too, and tears the location's points up so you can drive it again from the start — the stages stay open in the time trial; the points, and the rungs they opened, go. The stage the campaign would pick up at is the one wearing a ring on the grid: it is where the controller's cursor lands, and START drives it.

- **Time trial** — the same stages behind a gate of its own, with your best time on each: a stage opens here once you have driven it to the FINISH LINE in the campaign — podium or not — because a time is something you chase on a road you have already seen the end of, and a stage you crossed the line on in ninth is exactly the road you now want the clock on. Nobody else is entered here: the trial is you and the clock. Your best run comes back out as a **ghost** — a see-through copy of the car you set it in, driving the stage again exactly as you drove it, while a chip beside the clock counts the metres of road between you. It cannot be touched: the ghost runs its own game, so there is nothing there to hit. Each stage also keeps a **high score table** — its ten best times, with three letters against each. Land on it and the results card asks for your initials the way a cabinet does: the slots come up holding the name you entered last (**AAA** the first time), typing anything at all replaces it from the first letter, and ENTER posts it. On a phone, tap a slot and type with the device keyboard; the active name stays above the keyboard. The board is only ever read at the line: there is no page listing every stage's times, because a board is an invitation to beat a time and an invitation is worth something with the road still in your hands.
- **Roam** — any seed at all. The stage pane shows the map that seed builds as an island of land around the route, turning slowly, with its lakes, hills and forest standing and the **route drawn on it in yellow** (green marks the start, red the finish), so you choose by looking rather than by reading a number. The map is yours to handle: **drag** it to turn it and to tilt it down toward the horizon — which is how you see what "alpine" actually did to it — **scroll or pinch** to zoom in, **hold ⌘/CTRL and drag** (or drag with two fingers, or the middle button) to walk it sideways, and **double-click** to put it back. Zoom keeps going: leaned all the way in the lens is a few metres off the ground, close enough to see the road surface, the trees beside it and the car parked on the start line, and the pan is what gets you to the part of the stage you wanted to look at. It picks its slow turn back up a few seconds after you let go — but only once you have zoomed back out to the whole stage, because a map you have leaned in on is a map you are looking at something on. Beside the map is one column of settings, every one of them the same row the Options page is built from — a mark, a name, and the value between two arrows. **DIFFICULTY** leads it, across the whole column, and it is the one slider on the page: drag it from GENTLE up through RALLY — the middle, and the road every campaign stage is built on — to SAVAGE, and the same seed builds a harder road. More of it is corner and more of those corners are tight ones, the jumps throw the car further, the country either side stands higher (which is what you fall down when you slide off the outside of one), and the road itself narrows. There is no ROAD WIDTH row any more; this is it. **STAGE** is the seed — the arrows step to the road next door, the number can be typed straight into, and the die beside it rolls one nobody has driven — the length band (short ≈1 minute, medium ≈3, long ≈5, extra long ≈7 — or **endless**, which streams new road from the seed for as long as you drive) and the **shape**: a SPRINT runs from a start line to a finish somewhere else, a CIRCUIT closes back onto its own start line and is raced over three laps — same minutes of driving either way, cut into laps instead of laid out in a line. **SKY** is the hour, the weather and the season. **LAND** is the country and what the generator makes of it — hills, terrain, water, forest, tarmac — with the map redrawing as you press one. Across the top of the column, **SELECT A LEVEL** puts one of the campaign's own roads on the map instead, off the same stage boxes the campaign uses: its seed, its band, its shape and the hour and weather it is authored in, all of which you may then change. **CHOOSE A CAR** at the foot goes on to the pre-race card, where the car is picked and the run starts.
- **Options** — the HUD, the renderer, and the controls. See below.

Picking a stage anywhere — the campaign, the time trial, heads up, or Roam — does not start it: it opens the **pre-race card**, and the car is what that card is. It turns on a stand that takes most of the screen, with its name across the top of the frame, its drive layout beside that, its one line of billing across the foot, and an arrow at each edge for the next car and the last. Beside it are two figures — **top speed** and **0–100** — and four bars: **acceleration**, **top speed**, **grip** and **drifting**. The bars compare the car with the rest of the roster rather than with zero, because what you actually want to know is which of the three is the quick one, which is the grippy one, and which one wants to be sideways. **TRANSMISSION** leads that column — AUTO shifts for you, MANUAL is the racing set, about 6% taller — and it is the same setting as Options → Driving → GEARBOX, offered where the question is really being asked: take the manual and the two figures count up or down to their new values while the bars slide under them. Then **START** — or **DRIVE IT**, off Roam, where the card is titled by the level on the map or by the bare seed.

**Time of day** (dawn, day, dusk, night) and **weather** (clear, rain, storm — weather sets the wind, and the wind leans on the car) are yours on Roam and authored into each campaign stage. Off the pre-race card you sit on the line under a rally start gantry — three reds filling a lamp a second, a tick on each, then all green and away.

### Controls

**Desktop (keyboard).** Every one of these is rebindable on the KEYBOARD page under Options — press a row, then the key you want.

| Key        | Action                                                             |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| ↑ / W      | Throttle                                                           |
| ↓ / S      | Brake — and reverse, stopped                                       |
| ← → / A D  | Steer                                                              |
| Space      | Handbrake — unsticks the rear                                      |
| E / X      | Shift up (manual car)                                              |
| Q / Z      | Shift down (manual car)                                            |
| Esc        | In-race menu — and BACK, in a menu                                 |
| C / V      | Camera: next of six angles                                         |
| M          | Back to the main menu                                              |
| R          | Back to the track — the last checkpoint, and the road from there   |
| B          | Restart stage                                                      |
| Enter      | Screenshot — kept in the main menu's GALLERY                       |
| Alt (hold) | Hide the HUD — for a clean look at the game, or a screenshot of it |

What R costs is the road back to the last split board. Before the first board
there is no board to go back to, so it is the START LINE — and that is the run
beginning again rather than a penalty inside it, so the car comes back whole:
the dents, the flats and whatever was left in the trees go with the attempt.
RESTART hands over a fresh car for the same reason.

**Phone (touch):** one half of the screen is the wheel — touch anywhere and drag sideways to steer. The rim has weight — it turns toward your thumb rather than snapping to it, so a thumb wobble is not a slide and a slight steer is a real option, while a committed shove takes it to full lock in about a quarter second. A blue arc fills the rim from 12 o'clock to the marker, showing how much lock the car has. The other half is the pedal: touching it is GAS, drag down to BRAKE, drag right for the handbrake (DRIFT — unsticks the rear for tight curves). Pulling the thumb back is the car being reined in, pushing it away is the car being sent — the gesture means what it looks like. Keep the brake held once the car has stopped and it backs up, so a nose in a tree is something you drive out of. Which half steers, and which drag does what, are set in Options → Controls; each direction holds one action, so assigning a taken one swaps them. **The manual box takes its gears from the same thumb**: a quick FLICK up is a gear up and a flick down is a gear down — stab off the anchor and straight back off the glass, without ever leaving the throttle for a button. What separates a flick down from the brake bound to the same drag is that a brake is a HOLD: stay down past a quarter second and it is the pedal, and letting go of it costs no gear. A flick only takes a gear the car can use — up once the shift light is on, down once the revs have dropped far enough for the lower ratio to hold the speed — so a stray stab costs nothing. **Two arrows sit just left of your thumb**, up and down, and the one that is lit gold is the gear that is there to take: no need to try it to find out. A key or a pad shoulder is never held to that: those are deliberate, and they shift whenever you ask. A **shutter** button sits at the left of the top bar — the phone's way to the screenshot the keyboard takes with Enter, and it is only offered where there is no keyboard to take one with. Beside it is the **back-to-the-track** button, on every device, because it is what you reach for with the car on its roof and that is the worst moment to be remembering a key. On a phone there is a second way to it: **swipe in from the left edge of the screen** — start on the bezel and pull the mark all the way across. It takes a long, level drag on purpose, because what it does costs you the road back to the last board. Tap the **minimap** for the in-race menu (resume, options, restart, main menu). Works in portrait and landscape — the HUD re-flows to fit.

**Controller (any platform).** Plug in or pair a pad — an Xbox or DualSense controller on a desktop, a phone with a clip-on, or a handheld like a Retroid Pocket that runs the installed PWA on its own buttons — and press one of its buttons. It appears on the CONTROLLER page under Options, and the on-screen wheel and pedal come off the screen while it is connected.

| Control            | Driving                                                 | In a menu |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| Left stick         | Steer — analogue, so a small lean is a small correction | Move      |
| D-pad              | Steer, for anyone who would rather not use the stick    | Move      |
| R2 (right trigger) | Throttle — analogue: half a trigger is half the pedal   |           |
| L2 (left trigger)  | Brake — and reverse, stopped                            |           |
| A                  | Handbrake — unsticks the rear                           | Select    |
| B                  | Back to the track (off-road)                            | Back      |
| X                  | Camera: next of six angles                              |           |
| L1 / R1            | Shift down / up (manual car)                            |           |
| Select             | Screenshot                                              |           |
| Start              | In-race menu — and the way back out of it               | Next      |

The **analogue triggers** are the reason a pad is worth driving on: a slide is caught with a fraction of the throttle, which no key can express.

**The menus work on the pad too** — the stick and the d-pad walk the cards, A presses what the cursor is on, B is the way back. A gold ring shows where the cursor is, and holding a direction repeats it. It covers every surface: the main menu, the pre-race card, options, the pause card, the finish card and the results sheet.

**Start is NEXT**, and it is not the same press as A. A presses whatever the cursor is standing on; Start takes the screen's own way on, wherever the cursor happens to be — so pressing it from the front door goes CAMPAIGN, then the stage you are up to, then START, and you are on a start line without having chosen anything. Every card also stands the cursor where it makes sense to start: the campaign tile on the main menu, the stage you are up to on a ladder, the car itself on the pre-race card (left and right over the stand change the car, and the arrows either side of a value work that way everywhere — the seed on Roam, the volume faders in Options), and RESUME on the pause card. At the end of a run Start takes the next stage.

Two actions ship **unbound** on purpose, and for the same reason in each case — there is already a way to do it that cannot be done by accident. **RESTART STAGE**, because it is the one press on the pad that pressing again will not undo. **MAIN MENU**, because Start opens a card that has MAIN MENU on it, and a button that walks out of a run mid-run is the same fumble wearing a different hat. Both are in the list if you want them somewhere.

Everything here is rebindable the same way the keys are: tap a row, press the button. If the car wanders down a straight with nobody touching the stick, raise **stick deadzone**.

### Options

One page off the main menu, a dozen rows, every one the same shape: the setting's name on the left, its value on the right between two arrows, and pips under the value for where on its ladder it stands. Sideways is the only thing to learn — a mouse clicks the arrows, a thumb taps them, a pad's stick or d-pad presses them from wherever the cursor is — and the one sentence on the page, in the bar under the rows, explains whichever row you are on. Every change applies the moment it is made; RESTORE DEFAULTS at the bottom puts everything back. The page is not offered mid-stage. What the **pause card** carries instead is the handful of knobs you actually stop for — CAMERA, HUD, REAR VIEW and the two volumes — and a camera picked there lands on the car at once rather than at the next start line.

**HUD** is one switch: on, the whole instrument panel — clock, minimap, position, dials, the corner signs (each one the shape of the corner it calls, on a plate pointed the way the road goes), name tags and the top bar's buttons; off, a clean frame with only the pause button left in the corner, the start lights, the results card and the arrow that gets a lost car home. **REAR VIEW** is its own switch, because the mirror is the car's rather than the panel's — it stays up with the HUD off if you want it.

The **camera** key (C or V) walks eight angles, from the nose backwards: **BUMPER** down at the nose with no bodywork in frame at all, **HOOD** over your own bonnet, **COCKPIT** behind the wheel, then CLOSE, CHASE, FAR, HELI and TOP. OPTIONS ▸ DRIVING picks which one every stage opens on (the pause card carries the same row, so a view you cannot see out of is one press away mid-stage), and its **HEAD MOTION** switch is whether the driver's head moves at all inside the car. The head has weight: it is thrown forward under the brakes, sideways through a corner, down into a landing and along whatever you just hit, with the road buzzing up through it. HEAD MOTION OFF bolts the camera to the car for anybody the movement does not agree with.

**In the wet, the cockpit is a different stage.** From behind the wheel the windscreen is weather rather than a clean pane: water beads on it, the beads that break loose run and leave trails, and everything you can see through one is bent through it. Standing on the line it creeps down the glass; at speed the air over the scuttle drags it up, drawn out into tears, and a corner leans the runs toward the outside of the bend. Your wiper clears exactly the arc it sweeps and no more — the corners it cannot reach stream for the whole stage — and in a downpour the glass is beading again before the stroke has finished, so the road comes and goes with the arm. The door windows get the same rain and no wiper: on the grid they bead, and once you are rolling everything on them is blown back along the car in long horizontal streaks. It is the driver's view only, and it rides the same OPTIONS ▸ PICTURE ▸ DETAIL setting as the dirt on everyone's windows: on LOW the glass stays clean.

The **rear view** is a strip of glass at the top of the frame showing the road behind, from the car, left-for-right the way a mirror does — and from the CAR whichever angle you are watching from, so switching to the helicopter view does not turn the mirror round with it. From the COCKPIT it is not a strip at all: the same picture is in the mirror hanging in the top of your windscreen, so it swings with the car and moves as your head does. It is a second pass over the stage and the most expensive thing on this list, so it is also the first one to switch off on a device that is struggling — though the game gets there first: the glass is redrawn sixty times a second while the machine is holding sixty frames, and quietly less often (and, in the end, seeing less far) while it is not, so what a slow machine loses is the freshness of the mirror rather than the pace of the road. You can also put it out without leaving the stage: **click or tap the glass** and the picture stops, leaving the mirror where it was with REAR VIEW MIRROR OFF floating on the grey; tap that same grey and the road behind comes back. The mirror never moves and never shrinks, so switching it on is the same tap in the same place as switching it off — which is what makes it worth reaching for on a phone. That lasts as long as the session and nothing else — the HUD switch here is the one that takes the mirror off for good, and turning it back on puts the picture back with it. The **camera button** is only the on-screen one; the camera key (C or V) keeps working with it hidden.

**Screenshots.** Enter — or the shutter on the top bar, on a phone — takes a picture of the frame you are looking at, signed with the game's own mark in the corner. It is the whole screen: the car, the road, the weather, the mirror, and the instruments over them — the clock you were chasing and the call you took the corner on are half of what a rally picture is worth. For the frame on its own, hold **Alt** as you press (the chrome is off for as long as the key is down), or switch the HUD off for good in Options → HUD. Every picture goes into **GALLERY** on the main menu, where one shot fills the page and the roll runs as a filmstrip under it — the arrow keys or the chevrons flip between them. From there a picture can go to your phone's own share sheet, onto the clipboard, or into your downloads; only the ones your browser can actually do are offered. The roll keeps the last forty and the oldest falls off. Every picture also goes straight onto the **clipboard** as it is taken, so sending one is a paste rather than a trip through the gallery — Options → Controls → COPY TO CLIPBOARD turns that off on its own. Options → Controls → SCREENSHOTS switches the key and the shutter off; both ship on, and the gallery stays reachable either way.

**Picture** is three rows, because a slow frame has three different causes and you may not want to pay for the fix to all of them. **RESOLUTION** — LOW, MEDIUM or HIGH — is the pixel-ratio ceiling: LOW draws fewer pixels than your screen has and scales them up (soft, and the biggest single win on a weak phone), MEDIUM is one for one, HIGH goes up to two per screen pixel where the display has them. **DISTANCE** — NEAR, NORMAL or FAR — is how far the fog lets you see, which is the same thing as how much stage gets drawn: everything past the fog is dropped before the frame is built. NEAR is a real cut and looks like one — a clear day closes in around two hundred metres instead of five hundred — though it will not shorten the view past about five seconds of road, so it cannot stack with a downpour into a wall. **DETAIL** — LOW, MEDIUM or HIGH — is how much world gets built: the effects budget (dust, spray, exhaust, rain and the ambient life), how much cabin is behind every car's glass (LOW puts solid windows back and keeps every screen clean; the two above it furnish the trim, seats, crew, cage and a wheel that turns, and wet the glass for the wipers), the undergrowth between the trees, the loose stone spilled across the road's edge, and which cars raise dust off the stage (LOW takes the towed cloud, the wheel kickup and the rooster tail off every car on the road; MEDIUM leaves them to the car you are driving; HIGH gives the whole field its own plume, which is what a rally looks like from a corner away), and which cars SMOKE (LOW takes the exhaust off the road entirely; MEDIUM leaves the pipe on the car you are driving; HIGH puts a whole grid's worth of it on the start line). The last two walk the same ladder and are asked separately, because rain settles dust and does nothing at all to an exhaust.

MEDIUM on all three is the picture the game was tuned on, and the three are independent on purpose: a phone with a dense screen and a modest GPU wants HIGH resolution with NEAR distance and LOW detail, and a laptop pushing a big low-density panel wants the opposite. Resolution, distance, the dust and the exhaust take hold the moment you set them; the cabin and both scatters are baked into geometry, so those parts of DETAIL apply to the next stage you start. All of it is picture and nothing more: the trees you can HIT are always drawn, and nothing the ground loses is anything you could hit, so LOW never makes a stage easier. How thickly the forest itself stands is a stage dial, set per run in Roam.

**Controls** only ever offers what the device you are on can use: a touch device gets which thumb steers, a desktop gets a KEYBOARD row that opens the bindings, a laptop with a touchscreen gets both, and the CONTROLLER row opens the moment a pad is plugged in. **Driving** holds the camera a stage opens on and the HEAD MOTION switch. The gearbox is not here: AUTO or MANUAL is asked on the pre-race card, where the car's top speed and 0–100 are quoted through the box you are choosing, and whichever you picked last is the one the next car is offered with.

### Developer tools

Tap the car on its stand seven times and a **DEVELOPER** tile appears on the main menu; it stays for good. Besides opening every stage, it holds the two tools that make a problem you can SEE into one somebody else can reproduce — both also on the pause card mid-run, which is where you actually want them:

- **God mode** takes the camera off the car and flies it, on whatever the game is being played on. On a keyboard: `W A S D` to move (forward follows where you look; strafe stays level), `Space`/`E` up, `Ctrl`/`Q` down, `Shift` for a burst of speed, `-` and `=` for the cruise speed, the arrow keys or the mouse (click first) to look. On a **controller**: the stick you steer with moves the rig, the other one aims it, the triggers climb and descend, the handbrake button is the burst of speed, and the shoulders you shift with turn the cruise speed. On a **touchscreen**: the driving zones become fly zones — a push stick under the steering thumb, drag-to-look under the other, and a row of buttons for up, down and the cruise speed. **The run is HELD for as long as you are flying**: the clock stops, the field stops, the weather stops, and the car waits exactly where you left it — flying is for looking at a moment, and a moment that drove on while you looked at it is one you cannot fly back to. Switch god mode off and the run picks up from the same instant.
- **Debug overlay** puts boxes in the corner naming the stage, where the camera is standing, and what the car is doing — and along the bottom, a **REPRO** line: a URL carrying the seed, the generator dials, the conditions and the camera's exact pose. Send a screenshot with that line in it and whoever reads it can open the same frame. Holding Alt hides the game's HUD and leaves these boxes up, which is the shot worth sending. **The boxes go into the picture, not just onto the screen**: with the overlay up, a screenshot has the panels and the REPRO line painted into its pixels, so the file still says where it was taken after it has been pasted into a chat window. Flying with the overlay OFF — which is what you want when the whole frame is the thing being judged — there is a **COPY DEBUG INFO** button in the bottom corner instead: one press and the same facts are on the clipboard as text.

**DEVELOPER → DEBUG LOG** keeps what led there: every engine event with its numbers, and a position line a second. Copy the latest run or the whole buffer.

Once that tile is out, the **frame rate** is also on the HUD for good — under the minimap, beside the stage number, where it can be read while the game is actually being played rather than from behind a wall of debug boxes. The overlay's CAMERA box carries the same number plus what the rear-view mirror is currently costing.

### Installing on your phone

The game is a PWA. iOS Safari: Share → **Add to Home Screen**. Android Chrome: menu → **Install app** (or accept the install prompt). The installed game launches fullscreen, works offline once loaded, and shows an in-app prompt when a new build ships.

### Installing on your computer

Every [release](https://github.com/niclaslindstedt/game2/releases) carries the same game as a desktop app: a `-setup.exe` for Windows, a `.dmg` for macOS (`aarch64` for Apple Silicon, `x64` for an Intel Mac), a `.deb` or `.AppImage` for Linux. It is the website in a window of its own — offline, remembering its size and place, with F11 or Alt+Enter for fullscreen — and it updates when you install the next release. On macOS the first launch is refused, because the app is signed but not notarized: open System Settings → Privacy & Security, find the message about Scandinavian Flick and choose **Open Anyway**.

### How a run works

Stages are point-to-point: start gate to finish gate. The cluster in the bottom-left is the instrument panel: rev counter, gear with a shift light, and speed. Hold the engine on its limiter and the rev counter shakes. On the face of the rev counter are two **counters**, the pair a car's own cluster carries. The lower one is the TRIP: this stage, from zero, wound back the moment you start another. The upper one is the car's whole life — six kilometre drums, so it reads to 999999 km, the better part of a hundred thousand miles. The trip steps a figure every hundred metres and the total every kilometre, and on both the drum on the end turns continuously with the wheels the way a real one does, so there is always something moving down there. The total belongs to the CAR rather than to the run: every kilometre you drive it anywhere — the campaign, a time trial, a heads-up race, Roam, the training ground — is on it for good, and each car in the garage keeps its own. What the car has broken is not drawn anywhere — you can see the dents, and the machinery under them tells you itself, in the middle of the screen: ENGINE DAMAGED as it starts to give, ENGINE BROKEN once it has gone.

The **clock** owns the top-left corner, because in a racing game the clock is the opponent: TOTAL TIME big, and under it — on a circuit — the LAP TIME with the lap counter beside it, then the laps already set this run and the record the stage is holding for you. The **minimap** sits top-right: the country around you seen from above, with your car in the middle of it, travelling with you wherever you go — down the road, through a field, up a lane nobody taped off. It is a cartoon of the game rather than a picture of the stage: the road you are on as a pale ribbon with its tarmac stretches picked out, every other road beside it, the water, the clearings you could get through the forest by, and the buildings as blocks. It breathes with the speedo, so what is in the frame is about the same amount of NOTICE whatever the car is doing: picking your way through a farmyard it closes right in and becomes a plan of the ground under the wheels, and wound up on a straight it pulls back to most of half a kilometre of road. The country visibly opening out as you accelerate is the map telling you how fast you are going without a number on it. Under the frame is how much stage is left; the frame itself is the progress gauge, filling clockwise from the top as you get further in (on a circuit it fills once per lap and reads LAP 2/3; an endless run has no finish to fill toward, so it reads the distance covered instead). The split board you still owe is ringed in yellow while it is on the map and points at you from the rim of it while it is not — which is the mark to steer at when the road is nowhere in sight. In a HEADS UP race the window opens wider still, because there the map also carries the race: every rival near you is a plate with their door number on it, painted the colour of their car, so you can watch the order change instead of waiting to be told about it — and the leader's plate is always the one on top. Tap or click it for the in-race menu — resume, restart, or back to the main menu. The stage number under the map is the seed. On a campaign stage the **position board** sits between the camera button and the map, as big as the clock is — your place out of the fifteen crews entered, as of the last split board you went through; a heads-up race left on one green, so there it counts live. Finishing puts your time on screen; a campaign stage records the clear when the place was good enough for the podium, and the points that clear paid are what open the next one. The card also says what the place was worth, where the location's table stands after it, and carries the whole field's classification, paged, with your own row lit. That sheet is not final until the last car is home: while they are still coming the crews still out sit at the bottom of it marked OUT, the foot of the card counts them — and the card stands over the race that is still going on. You get the flying finish first, the camera planted at the gate watching you go; a few seconds down the run-out it leaves your car and watches the crew behind you instead, from high up, the way the main menu watches a stage. **SPECTATE** takes you down to them: the camera flies to the car in a second, in an arc over whatever country is in the way, and lands in the chase view you raced under — and the card comes down for the HUD you just raced under, reading THEIR car. Their clock top left, and under it how many seconds they are up or down on you at the last split you have both been through; their place and their route top right; their revs, gear and speed bottom left. Above the car, where the co-driver's calls would be, a banner names the crew, and the arrows either side of it walk the field a car at a time. It cuts to the next car by itself when the one you are watching crosses the line. BACK TO RESULTS puts the card up whenever you have seen enough and the race carries on behind it. The classification is the same one either way — and pressing on to the next stage before the last car is home still records it in full.

**Off the road is a real place.** The landscape around every stage is driveable — forests, hills, mountains, streams, lakes and open sea — and the car rides it fast (up to ~150 km/h in the wild; ~230 km/h flat out on the road). Cliff edges and banks throw the car if you hit them with pace; boulders and fallen trunks are genuinely solid, and deep water swallows the car whole. A crash puts you back on the track where you left it. There is no off-road timer: explore as long as you like, and press **B** when you want back.

**Drifting** is not a button — it is just what the car does when you turn harder than the tires can hold, which past about 70 km/h is any committed turn. Steer into the bend and the car goes sideways while the road keeps flowing: steering into it deepens it, lifting the throttle tightens it, counter-steering gathers it up. It costs you almost no speed, so there is nothing to count and nothing to cash in — just keep it flowing. The handbrake is there to unstick the rear in something really tight. **Jumps** throw you off ramps; midair the car barely answers, so line up before the lip and land straight or lose speed. **Fords** splash and drag; **crests** can go light at full speed.

```sh
git clone https://github.com/niclaslindstedt/game2
cd game2
npm install        # needs a GitHub Packages token — see docs/configuration.md

```
