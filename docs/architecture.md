# Architecture

The repository follows the shape of its sibling game repo: a headless engine that IS the game, a thin browser shell that draws it, and tooling that measures it. One direction of dependency:

```
tests/  scripts/(sim, previews)          pwa/ (Preact + three.js shell)
        \                                 /
         `----------->  engine/  <-------'
                 (framework-free TypeScript)
```

## `engine/` — the game, headless

The engine is a pure TypeScript module with no framework, no renderer, and no DOM. Its public surface is `engine/index.ts`:

- `createGame({ seed, carId })` builds a run: the compiled stage and a car on the grid.
- `step(state, input)` advances one fixed 120 Hz timestep (`TUNING.physicsHz`) and returns the `GameEvent[]` that step emitted (`takeoff`, `landing`, `splash`, `shift`, `respawn`, `finish`, …). The browser's render loop and the headless simulator call this same function — there is no other way to advance a run.

Determinism is a hard invariant: everything random draws from the seeded RNG in the state (`engine/lib/prng.ts`), never `Math.random`, so a seed fully reproduces a stage and a bot run — which is what the sim digests and shareable daily stages rely on.

## `pwa/` — the browser shell

A Vite + Preact app that renders the engine and ships it as an installable PWA:

- `src/game/renderer.ts` — the three.js facade: builds a `World` per stage (road ribbon with start/finish dirt aprons — `endApron` in the engine, and none on a circuit, whose ends are the lap itself — rumble strips, dirt skirts, fords, rally gates with hay bales), the car, dust particles, and the camera. It reads `GameState`; it never steps physics. A stage is not raised in one go: the road goes up a slice at a time and the ground a few tiles at a time, on the frames after it (`world.sync`), so changing a setting in Roam grows a new stage in instead of freezing the app while it is born. The world is lit by the environment's own hemisphere and sun (`environment.ts`, over `daylight.ts` for where the sun is, `sky.ts` for the colours, `sky-shader.ts` and `cloud-field.ts` for what is in the sky — `clouds.ts` on the LOW setting — `height-fog.ts` for the mist and the shadows in the air, and `storm.ts` for the electrics) and vertex-colored with procedural speckle textures (`textures.ts`); the CAR is LIT BY THE SCENE, by the same hemisphere and sun as the ground it stands on (`car-surface.ts`, a `MeshPhongMaterial` over the flat face normals `car/builder.ts` writes). Its colour attribute is albedo with no shading folded into it, and the highlight is what makes it read as painted metal rather than a coloured shape — one shininess for the whole car, and a per-vertex gloss weight (`aShine`, `SHINE`) grafted onto the specular term so a lacquered wing and a rubber tyre can differ while staying one material and one draw call. On top of that sits `car-glow.ts`, which is the one light the scene cannot throw for itself: a headlamp is aimed down the road, so the real spotlight behind it never touches the wing carrying it, and the spill around each lens is authored from a register of the car's own lamp anchors. It is added as LIGHT rather than into the albedo — folded into the albedo it would be multiplied by a night sky of nearly nothing and vanish on the frames it exists for — with a third of it as gloss so a dark car reads as lit too. The cabin's furniture carries a material of its own to stay out of that wash: a lamp bolted to the outside of a closed box does not light the room behind it. The one real shadow in the scene is the cars' (`car-shadow.ts`): a shadow map hung off the sun that ONLY the cars are drawn into. WHO READS IT is the LIGHTING row's call (`RICH_SHADOWS`). Up to `normal` only the ground does — terrain, road, water — so the lookup is paid for the ground alone and, because the ground is never in the map, there is no acne to bias away. At `full` the cars read it too: a roof shades its own door and an arch its own tyre. That puts a receiver inside the map and brings the self-shadow acne with it, so that stop alone turns on a `normalBias` sized off the map's own texel. `full` also lets the FOREST cast — but never by flagging the world's own flora, which is instanced per chunk and, on a finite stage, is one mesh spanning kilometres that can never be culled out of the pass. `flora-shadow.ts` keeps a pool instead: at most a handful of species, at most a few dozen plants each, refilled from the live chunks as the car moves. That makes the top stop cost a FIXED amount on a stage of any length or density — measured at no change in draw calls and about sixteen thousand triangles. The frame follows the car the camera is on, biased up the road, and is snapped to its own texels so a still car keeps the same edge from frame to frame. `sunHardness` in `sky.ts` says how much of the light is a beam at all: under a storm's ceiling nothing is cast, under a thin sheet of cloud a faded one is. The map's size — and whether there is one — rides the video options' LIGHTING row (`SHADOW_MAP_SIZE`), the same row that decides how many spotlights the car's lamps throw (`LAMP_BEAMS`), because a spotlight is evaluated by every lit pixel in the frame and is the one cost that scales with the picture rather than with what is in it. That is why it is a row of its own beside DETAIL rather than a lever on it: thinning the world makes everything on DETAIL cheaper and makes this no cheaper at all, so a machine that cannot hold the lamps can still hold a furnished field in daylight. That row is a CAP on the lamps the car actually has rather than a fixed count: at the top it throws the whole complement its body authored (`headLampSources` in `car/lamps.ts` — a quad face's low beams AND its driving pair, a rally car's pod bar), plus a pair of BRAKE lights and the rivals' own lamps on the dust register; in the middle it throws the strongest pair at each end, the brakes with them, and nobody else's; at the bottom one headlamp beam on the centreline, no tail beam and so no brakes. Whichever it is, each end's light is a fixed budget shared out among the beams thrown, so the row buys the SHAPE of the pool and never how far a player can see. A brake light is a light of its own rather than the marker turned up — the marker is worth half of what it lights and the brakes twice that, thrown steeply into the strip of road a chase camera can actually see — and by day it is the bloom over the lens alone, because a spotlight competing with the sun changes no pixel and costs every one of them. WHEN any of it is lit has three stops rather than a switch (`LampStage` in `sky.ts`), and the two lit ones are two different LAMPS rather than one lamp at two brightnesses — because that is what a dip switch does: it does not dim a headlamp, it lights a different one. The sky sets the ceiling. Off in daylight; DIPPED from about three quarters of an hour before sunset, where the low beams alone burn — the `main`-role bowls `car/lamps.ts` already picks out per body — at a low beam's own optics: half the reach, a bit under half the light, a wider cone, aimed at the road (the ratios are the real ones — a low beam shows an obstacle at ~60 m against a main beam's ~120, and FMVSS 108 caps the upper beam at 75 000 cd at H-V against a low beam's 20–36 kcd). A driver switching on at that hour is doing it to be SEEN rather than to see. Then MAIN once the sun is under the horizon, where the driving lamps and the pod bar light with them, the nose gets its whole budget down the body's authored reach, and the blue tell-tale on the pod comes on — which is what that lamp means on every car ever built. Weather only ever raises the stop, off how much daylight is actually left on the road (`dayLight`) rather than off how thick the lid is, so a black storm at noon runs dipped beams and the same deck late in the afternoon runs main. Under all of it sits the DIP SWITCH (`dipFor` in `car-beams.ts`, the DOM-free half of the model and the one the tests hold): main beam is for a road with nobody on it, so a crew close enough ahead takes it back down to dipped and puts the pod bar out. What "close enough" means is not a constant but the car's own reach — past where the beams end there is nobody to dazzle — so a machine carrying a bar dips earlier than one on a pair of sealed beams, with a band around the threshold so a rival sitting on it cannot switch the whole field's materials every frame. The paint takes the hour from the lights now, but the lenses and the cabin still do not — they are unlit by design, because a lamp gets BRIGHTER as the light goes and a tint is the wrong direction for it: `environment.onRelight` carries the stop over to them, at the sky's own re-read cadence and again the instant it moves. The rough arcade look is deliberate.
- **What a frame costs, and how it is kept down.** The renderer's budget is DRAW CALLS before it is triangles: a stage is thousands of separate things, and a car alone was forty of them before its wheels were welded into one mesh each. Four rules hold the count down, and `make profile` is how any change to them is judged. (1) _Nothing is built twice_ — every procedural texture, the flora library's shapes and the two materials it plants them with are made once and shared by the whole app (`lib/shared-gpu.ts` marks them, and every teardown path skips what is marked, because a chunk being dropped must not free what the chunks still standing are drawn with). (2) _Anything repeated is instanced_ — flora, stone litter, the crowd and the sky's clouds are all one draw per kind, not one per thing. (3) _Nothing rigid is split_ — a wheel is one geometry, not a tire plus a rim plus eight lugs, because nothing ever moves one of them relative to another. (4) _Nothing invisible is submitted_ — the camera's far plane stands well past where the fog goes solid, so `world.cull` hides the road chunks and wild cells past the fog's own far distance, and the clouds cull themselves cluster by cluster (they orbit a ring the camera stands inside, so three's own test over the whole ring always answers yes).
- `src/game/biome.ts` / `biome-*.ts` / `flora*.ts` — the nature: biomes as data and the parametric low-poly flora library (~85 tree/cactus/shrub/dead-wood/ground-cover variants, authored at the height a mature one stands — a boreal canopy is 20–30 m up, with a rare old-growth giant near fifty — one instanced mesh per variant per patch of ground, wind-swayed grass and ferns). `biome.ts` is the types and the registry; each country is a file beside it (`biome-taiga.ts`, `biome-desert.ts`, `biome-alpine.ts`), and which one a stage is dressed in is the track's own dial (`track.knobs.biome`, R40 — the engine's `mapgen/biomes.ts` carries the same country's quilt, and the two are checked against each other at import). The library is split four ways — `flora-build.ts` (the primitives and the paint box, both countries' colours), `flora-species.ts` (the taiga's roster of recipes), `flora-desert.ts` (the desert's: saguaros, Joshua trees, mesquite, the cholla and the ocotillo, the tufts and the bones), `flora-alpine.ts` (the alpine's: arolla pine, the windswept larch, the krummholz, alpenrose and the flowers of the alp) and `flora.ts` (shapes, materials and instancing over the merged roster). **The landscape is quilted at three scales**, all of them placed in the ENGINE (`mapgen/props.ts`) because the trunks are collided with: a SUB-REGION (~900 m) says what kind of country this is — dense forest, open taiga, a logging block, a bog, an old burn — and re-weights the GROVES (~150 m) below it, while a STAND noise (~42 m) clumps the trees inside one grove into closed thickets and the clearings between them — and inside that, a tree cell grows a CLUMP of one to four stems rather than a single trunk, at no cost in density, because a grid that holds one trunk per cell can only ever produce grid spacing. The same field lays BLOWDOWNS (parallel fallen trunks down the fall line where a gale went through) and ROCKY OUTCROPS (five to ten stones bedded into a steep hillside along its contour). `biome.ts` maps those ids to species mixes, ground cover and per-region ground colors; `planting.ts` decides which species dresses each engine trunk and each engine prop (fallen trunks, stumps, timber stacks), with the water and the altitude overriding the quilt — shoreline, stream bank, highland; it also grows the UNDERSTORY, the skirt of saplings, junipers and moss that low growth keeps around the foot of a mature trunk. Two callers decide where: `world.ts` for the band each road chunk carries (planted as a unit, so the fog cull can hide it as one), `wild.ts` for the open country beyond it (pooled into one instanced mesh per variant across every cell standing, because a cell holds too few of any one plant to be worth a draw call of its own). A variant's shape is BUILT ONCE and shared by every patch that plants it — a handful of jittered builds each, from a cache keyed by variant, jitter AND SEASON — so a stage streaming in uploads a few dozen geometries rather than one per variant per chunk. All of it seeded by the track seed. The `nature` skill owns the details.
- **The weather.** `RaceEnv.weather` is clear, rain or storm, and the last two are not the first one dimmed by different amounts — they are a LID on the sky. `sky.ts` turns the conditions into a `Preset`, and a wet one carries a `Deck`: a ceiling a few hundred metres up whose UNDERSIDE (`clouds.ts` draws it, one mesh, one draw) is most of what the player can see. The two wet skies then move in opposite directions, which is what stops both collapsing into grey. Under RAIN the deck is thin enough to glow — near white overhead, greying toward the rim where the line of sight runs the long way through it, so a rainy day is a WHITE sky. Under a STORM it is kilometres thick and sits in its own shadow — nearly black overhead, with the only daylight left in the strip that gets in UNDER the base at the horizon, which is the gust-front look. The fair-weather cumulus ring does not disappear under either: it drops below the base and becomes SCUD, flattened, darkened and tearing along at two and a half times the pace, which is the only place the wind under a cloud base is visible. How heavy a given stage's weather is comes from `weather.ts`: `coverOf` reads where the stage's own seeded wind sits inside the band its weather allows (`TUNING.wind.speed`), so no two wet stages get the same ceiling and the same seed always brings the same one back; `squallOf` reads the LIVE gust against that mean, so the sheet of rain thickens exactly as the car is shoved sideways — one gust, felt, seen and heard. A heavy deck also takes the daylight down far enough that the car turns its lights on at noon. Every colour of the AIR in all of this is a colour DAYLIGHT makes — the lid's grey, the lit strip under the gust front, and the season's own cast over the clear sky (September's warm horizon band, winter's ice haze) — so each of them rides one ramp on the sun's elevation (`daytime` in `sky.ts`): full while the sun is up, gone by nautical twilight. Left at full strength after dark they were not merely wrong but violently so, because `mixHex` mixes in LINEAR light: a fifth of the way from a midnight sky toward a bright authored grey lands two thirds of the way up the sRGB ramp, so a night storm came out with a daylight-grey horizon under a ceiling drawn black.

- **The ridges on the horizon.** `horizon.ts` closes the view with four camera-locked silhouette rings — the farthest snow-capped, the nearest a serrated treeline — with a sea gap turned to this run's sunrise or sunset, and the whole chain is ONE mesh whose vertex colours are repainted when the conditions change. What each vertex is painted is the sky BEHIND IT, read at that vertex's own elevation: the dome's distance fog under an open sky, and under a deck the ceiling's own ramp from its lit rim to its black underside (`deckToneAt`, stated once in `sky.ts` because both skies draw the ceiling and this reads it too). It has to be per vertex because the rings run from the skyline to about twenty degrees while the rim band is the lower nine (`RIM_BAND`) — so the sky's steepest gradient crosses the chain, and one flat colour for a whole ring came out darker than the sky at its feet and many times brighter than the sky at its summits, which reads as a cut-out laid over the weather. Read per vertex the range picks the gradient up instead: its feet dissolve into the lit gap under the base and its tops go to soot. The SNOW is not the rock turned up — it is a white surface, so it is the colour of the light on it (`Preset.sun` at the root of `dayLight`): warm at noon, orange under a low sun, a dim blue under the moon, and grey under a storm. A multiply is what that used to be, and a multiply over one OVERFLOWS — a peak painted at 1.7x a sunset's peach clipped its red channel, lost the hue with it and came back chalk-white against an orange sky. `tests/sky_test.ts` holds the rule the whole thing exists for: rock never stands brighter than the sky behind it, and the chain is dark at midnight in every weather.

- **The sun, and the clock it runs on.** A stage is set to an HOUR (`RaceEnv.hour`), and the sun moves from it at an hour a minute of racing (`sunHourAt`, `SUN_SECONDS_PER_HOUR`): where it stands is real astronomy — `daylight.ts` takes the hour, the season's declination and the country's latitude (R40) and hands back an elevation, a bearing and which way it is going — so 16:00 in a taiga winter is night, a midsummer night at 62°N never gets past civil twilight, and the sun rises in the north-east in June and the south-east in December. `sky.ts` is a LADDER over that elevation rather than four authored presets: rungs for the dark, civil twilight, the sun on the horizon, the golden hour, the morning and the day, with the dawn rungs painted differently from the dusk ones (peach and mist against magenta and orange), blended so a stage driven from sunset into the night never cuts; the key light hands over from the sun to a full moon dead opposite it as the sun drops through twilight, and never goes under the ground. The environment re-reads the sky a few times a second off the race clock (`RELIGHT_EVERY`), re-lights everything hung on it, turns the horizon's rings so their sea gap faces this run's sunrise or sunset (`horizonCrossing`), and dims the beam when the sun drops behind the ridge on its bearing or a cumulus crosses it. The four words the audio and the menu key on (`Daylight`) are that elevation binned; a stored setting or a `?tod=` link written as a word is read as the hour that light happens at (`hourOfWord`). `make sky` is a September day over the taiga, hour by hour.
- **What is in the sky, at height.** On the MEDIUM and HIGH stops of the SKY lever (`SKY_LOOK`) the dome is one shader (`sky-shader.ts`): the gradient and the glow, the stars, the disc and halo, and the CLOUD LAYERS of `cloud-field.ts` — each a sheet of noise at a real altitude over the sea (the cloud chart's bands: cumulus and stratocumulus low, altocumulus in the middle, cirrus combed along the wind at nine kilometres, and combed again into FIBRES — the hair-like filaments a cirrus reads by, a fine field across the wind and long along it that thickens the veil where it already is (`cloudFibres`, faded out toward the rim where they would be under a pixel) — lit through from every side because ice that thin has no shaded face) projected onto the view ray in perspective, so a cumulus foreshortens into the haze and a cirrus sheet hardly moves as the car does, and an alpine pass at six hundred metres looks up at the cumulus and down onto the valley. Each stage is DRESSED from its seed for its country and season (`dressSky`): a summer forest builds cumulus, a winter one sits under a stratocumulus sheet, the desert has a few heaps two kilometres up and cirrus, the Alps build over the peaks and comb altocumulus into lenticular streaks. Some days it makes NOTHING — a settled airmass builds no cloud, and the stage is driven under bare blue from one horizon to the other (`CLOUDLESS`, likeliest over the desert and rarest over the forest), which the LOW sky honours too by flying none of its ring; a sheet too thin to draw is dropped rather than spending a layer of the budget on it. Rain and a storm are a deck coloured overhead-to-rim by elevation exactly as the simple sky's mesh is, dark at night, with scud tearing under it, and the desert's storm is blown sand. The sun sets on the layers lowest first (`litAt`: the horizon dips with height, so a sheet ten kilometres up is in sun for a quarter of an hour after the ground), which is what makes a sunset sky and lights the contrails orange after the valley has gone grey. The noise lives twice — GLSL for the dome and the ground, the same arithmetic in TypeScript — so the environment reads, on the CPU, how much cloud is between the car and the sun (`sunOcclusion`) and dims the key light and softens the cars' shadows by it; `tests/cloud_field_test.ts` holds the two copies to the same constants. The LOW stop keeps `clouds.ts`: the vertex dome, the ring of puffs and the mesh deck.
- **The night sky.** There is no light pollution anywhere in this game — every stage is a hundred kilometres from a town — so a clear night is a dark-sky night, and `starfield.ts` is what stands in it. The stars are on a sphere that WHEELS: its pole stands at the country's own latitude (R40, the same number the sun's arc comes off), the hour spins it, and the season offsets it a whole turn a year — so at an hour of sun a minute of racing a stage watches the sky turn, and midwinter at midnight looks out at a different part of it than late September does. Across that sphere lies the MILKY WAY, on the real galactic frame: a great circle tilted 63° to the celestial pole, so the band comes up steeply out of one horizon rather than ringing the sky parallel to it. It carries a bulge toward the centre, mottling from the same noise the clouds are cut from, and the Great Rift — a dark lane of dust down the brightest part of it — with three faint elliptical smudges (the other galaxies) off it. The stars are two grids over that sphere: about four hundred and fifty on a coarse one and six thousand on a fine one, each with a magnitude, a colour off the spectral sequence, and a scintillation that grows toward the horizon where the line of sight runs the long way through the air. They are SIZED IN PIXELS and not in radians — a star drawn at an angular size is under a pixel on a contact sheet and a blob at 1080p — which is measured off the view ray's own derivative. Three things take the night sky away: the air at the horizon (five airmasses at the rim take most of a star and all of a galaxy), the moon's own sky glow (a wide wash round the key, which is what leaves the bright stars and kills the band), and the CLOUD in front — the sheets are drawn over it, so a thick one takes it, a thin one hints it, and a deck is a lid. How much band a sky has at all is authored on the same ladder the colours are, but on a far steeper curve (`galaxy` cubed in `clearSky`): the band is a glow a shade over the sky's own floor, and a twilight sky is nowhere near that floor. Both skies read the one chart — the dome takes the turn as a change of basis on its ray and evaluates the band per pixel; the LOW setting's `night-sky.ts` bakes the same field into a couple of thousand points and one additive strip, at a share of the light because its own dome's midnight is a much lighter sky. `tests/starfield_test.ts` holds the two to the same sphere.
- **The mist, and the shadows in the air.** `mist.ts` is a rule: radiation fog lies in the valleys on a clear night, thickest around sunrise, burnt off by mid-morning, most in autumn and none in a dry desert — a sheet over the taiga's bogs, a CLOUD SEA in the Alps that a pass looks down onto. `height-fog.ts` grafts it onto three's own fog chunk, once, before the first material compiles: every fogged material carries the world position down and integrates the mist along the ray (a closed form, split at the sheet's top), colours it lit or shaded with the sun's glow toward the sun, and the dome draws the same sea over the same functions so there is no seam at the fog's far edge. The uniforms are PLAIN OBJECTS shared by reference — three clones a material's uniforms and refreshes only the fog's own four, and a literal `{x, y, z, w}` survives the clone where a vector is copied. `mountain-shadow.ts` marches the stage's heightfield toward the sun over a window round the camera and writes, per cell, the height below which the ground is in the country's shadow — a valley goes into the mountain's shade while the peaks across it are still lit, the sea in it is grey under an orange sky — and on HIGH the cumulus layer's field is read again at each ground fragment along the sun's direction for the clouds' shadows on the stage.

- **The sandstorm.** The desert's weather, and the only one that ARRIVES. The engine owns the front itself (`engine/game/sandstorm.ts` — a seeded schedule answerable at any time in any order, so replays, traces and endless runs all agree; see [driving.md](driving.md)); `sand-air.ts` is what it LOOKS like. Two halves, and the first is the point: the WALL, a curtain standing nine hundred metres up out at the horizon, upwind, rising and darkening across the approach and handing over to the sand itself the moment the front lands — a haboob announces itself for a minute before it takes you, and that minute is the whole feature. The second is the GRAINS, built like the rain (`rain.ts`) and drawn at the velocity they are SEEN at, but sand does not fall: it is carried, so it goes ACROSS the screen where rain comes at it, and a driver can read the crosswind off it. Everything else the storm does to the picture is the air: `environment.ts` collapses the fog range by what is left of the visibility (`sandVisibility`, which falls away far faster than the sand rises) and tints the fog and the sky over it to lit copper and dirty brown — a haboob blots the sun out from BELOW, so the top of the frame is the darker half, which is the opposite of every other weather here and the thing that makes it read.
- **The storm.** `storm.ts` owns the lightning and is deliberately not a light that blinks on a timer. Strikes come in CELLS (the rate itself breathes, so the quiet stretches are what make the next crack land) with gaps drawn from an exponential; each strike has a DISTANCE, which decides how bright it is, whether a forked channel is drawn at all or only the cloud lighting up from inside (sheet lightning), and — the one nobody fakes — how long the sound takes to arrive, at 343 m/s. A flash is several RETURN STROKES tens of milliseconds apart rather than one smooth decay, and it lights the world FROM the bolt's bearing rather than lifting the key that is already there. Reduced motion keeps the storm and takes away the strobe. The thunder is a CUE, not a `GameEvent`: the simulation has no weather in it, so the renderer says when a clap arrives and how far it came, and `soundForThunder` in the audio router decides what that sounds like. Reviewed with `make sky`, which renders every weather across a day, hour by hour, and waits for a near strike — a run screenshot can only ever show one sky, and a flash lasts a fifth of a second.

- **The high traffic.** The sky over a stage is a lived-in place, and `ambient-life.ts` over `sky-traffic.ts` is what says so: airliners crossing the wilderness a few hundred metres up, and the contrails they leave hanging there for two and a half minutes afterwards. Six or so come over during a two-minute stage, but the crossings are not the point — the WAKE is. A stage OPENS on four trails at spread ages (`open`), so the grid stands under somebody else's morning rather than under an empty sky that fills up over the first minute, and one code path lays a live trail and backfills a two-minute-old one because a crossing carries its own age. A trail is what a photograph of one is: TWO hair-lines at the wings, one behind each engine (`PUFF.cores`), that spread from three metres to twenty-four as they age (`puffWidth`) and so merge into one broad band a few hundred metres behind the aeroplane — and DILUTE as they spread, by the square of it (`puffFade`: the ice is shared out, and a wider sprite overlaps more of its neighbours), so the old band is faint where the fresh line is white and never outshines it; `trailRead` is what a band of any age adds up to on screen, and the test judges it on that. It is laid as a close-packed line of point sprites, thins out over its last hundred seconds, breaks into lumps along its length (`trailLump`) and creeps on a fraction of the wind aloft, tapering at both tips — a contrail that simply stops in mid-sky reads as a mark on the lens — and it stays STRAIGHT to the end: a trail that wanders is smoke. The aeroplane is a SPECK, nine metres of span, and it crosses slowly, because the lanes cannot go above the camera's far plane and those two are what the eye reads altitude off. The lanes and the chord are ONE decision, because between them they set the ELEVATION a trail is seen at: over the driver's head at forty degrees and down through the band a windscreen actually shows at either end, with the far ends running out past the ridge rings. Two rules carry the rest of it. It rides the camera with no parallax the way the clouds do — in HEIGHT too, so the lane is an altitude over the eye and a road four hundred metres up a massif still looks up at it — because at that height a stage is not long enough for parallax and pretending otherwise walks the trails down onto the mountains. And it is drawn UNDER the cumulus (`renderOrder`), because three sorts transparent objects by how far their origin is from the camera and a pool whose origin is the ground would otherwise paint half a kilometre of sky over the clouds in front of it. None of it is drawn under a deck at all: high traffic is above the weather, so an overcast stage cannot see any of it. Reviewed with `make traffic`, which is a contact sheet for the same reason `make sky` is — the claim is about a rate, and no single frame carries a clock.

- **The seasons, and the cold.** `RaceEnv.season` is spring, summer, autumn or winter — and winter is the one that reaches the WHEELS. `engine/game/climate.ts` states it once, DOM-free: a stage carries a CLIMATE on its compiled track (`Track.climate` — the season, and a temperature at the datum that falls with height at the country's lapse rate, read off the bedrock before any road or plant is laid on it), and the effective snowline is the lower of the country's own permanent line and the height the air freezes at. Above it every loose road sample is compiled as `snow`, with a per-sample `bite` the local temperature sets (glazed a degree under zero, sharp in deep cold — always between gravel and the old alpine ice), and the terrain field lays THE BLANKET beside the road: `heightAt` carries half a metre to a metre of snow the tiles are drawn at, `groundAt` sinks the car `CLIMATE.blanket.ride` of the way into it, and the physics calls what it is standing on there a `snowfield` — dragged, dug and soft. The season alone never moves a route: the same seed builds the same road in every season and only what it is made of changes, and every campaign level keeps its exact surfaces because the seasons' default temperatures hold the frost line over the country. Below freezing what falls is FLAKES (`snowfall.ts`, cross-faded with the rain at the camera's own height), which settle on the driver's glass and melt as the cabin warms (`car/screen-snow.ts`), and a car on snow leaves tracks (`snow-marks.ts`, on the DUST row's say). A season is not a filter: the year's colours are baked into the flora's vertices and the terrain's paint, so changing it re-plants the world (`renderer.setConditions` watches for it). What changes is what actually changes in a boreal forest — the broadleaves, the larch, the ground layer and the bogs — while every conifer, every bark and all the dead wood stay exactly where they are, which is what keeps the taiga's silhouette still while the colour moves. The LIGHT is derived rather than art-directed (`environment.ts`): at 62°N the noon sun stands 51° up at midsummer and 26° up at the September equinox, so the season scales the sun's elevation and the extra air mass its beam then travels through is charged against the Rayleigh optical depths of the three colour channels — which is why an autumn stage is warmer, dimmer and longer-shadowed before a single leaf has turned. The desert (R40) keeps the same three moments of the year at 33°N — so its September sun stands fifty degrees up, not twenty-six — and its spring is the loud season rather than its autumn: brittlebush yellow, white saguaro crowns, red ocotillo tips over a country that is grey-green the rest of the time. Winter's light is the cold air's: the deepest blue of the year and the longest view, the ground bouncing the sky back off the snow, and an ice haze closing the distance in again as the temperature falls.
- **The birds that are going somewhere.** Everything else that flies over a stage belongs to a place and turns in it — the flocks wheel round a point near the start, the buzzards and the eagles circle their thermals. `skein.ts` is the exception and is built on the opposite idea: a straight track, on a compass bearing, at a held altitude, with everybody in the formation going there together — a vee of geese, one straight trailing line of whooper swans, or a loose group between two lakes. THE SEASON IS THE POINT: in spring they come north and in autumn they go south (the compass is anchored to `SUN_AZIMUTH`, which stands in the south, because nothing else in this world has ever claimed a direction), high and in long vees; in summer the same birds are still here, lower, in smaller groups, on no particular bearing. The taiga and the Alps have them and the desert does not. Three details carry the look. A crossing is pitched over a point a few hundred metres ahead of where the camera is LOOKING and then backed a kilometre up its own bearing, so it arrives — a smudge, then a shape, then birds, then gone — and the first one in each slot is dropped part-flown so the grid stands under a sky that already has birds in it. The wingbeat runs DOWN the formation as a wave rather than striking together, which is the whole difference between a skein and a row of decorations. And the heights are the low end of what real birds do on purpose: at the three hundred metres geese are perfectly happy at, a skein is four pixels of grit on the lens. It is four draw calls whatever is in the sky — every bird is an instance of one wing and one body, and a beat is a rotation composed into its matrix. Heard as well as seen: `passageCalls` in `ambience.ts` puts the geese and the swans on the world's roster off the same season.

SPECTATE on the card is then the card coming down over that same shot rather than another flight — and the driving layout going back UP, pointed at the crew under the camera: their clock and their gap to the time already on the sheet top left, their place and their route top right, their revs, gear, speed and dents bottom left, all off a `takeSnapshot` of the rival's own `GameState`. The only chrome the mode adds is a banner naming them, in the co-driver's slot above the car (the pacenotes come down — there is nobody in that car to call a corner to), with the two arrows that walk the field on it and an automatic cut to the next crew when the one on screen comes home. Nothing about the result moves: every crew is driven alone whichever way the run-out is spent, and a time is that crew's own clock at their own line, so the sheet a spectator watches being decided is the sheet they would have been handed anyway — and a player who presses on before the road clears has it finished and booked for them on the way out. It also enters the OTHER kind of start: a HEADS-UP race hands `createField` a `FieldPlan` — how good the field is, how many cars are on it, and whether they leave together — and a MASS START stands them on a zig-zag grid on the apron behind the start gate (`engine/sim/grid.ts`) instead of in a queue, owing nobody a head start, sitting through the same lights the player does, and driving through the gate together on the green. The player takes the last slot, and the metres that costs come back as the game's only catch-up. The same module keeps the DERIVED start list the time trial and Roam still need, where nobody is entered and R25's finish salute still has to be sized by something.
- `src/game/campaign.ts` + `results-table.tsx` — R29/R30, the campaign, which IS its championship. The module holds the authored stages (a seed, a length band, a shape and conditions per level, so the same rules engine builds them for everybody) AND the points they are played for, in ONE localStorage record: every campaign stage pays 3/2/1 to its podium — the player and the fourteen rivals alike, off the classification `standings.ts` builds once the last car is home — and a location's table is those stages added up. There is no second board on top of it. The points ARE the locks: a stage opens once the one before it paid the player something (which is to say a podium), and a country opens once the one before it has been driven all the way through with the player top of its table (`locationWon`, which is what stops an all-square empty table from opening a country nobody has raced for). Nothing is ever spent: a stage can be re-run as often as the player likes for a better place, time and points, and the board keeps whichever afternoon paid them more, so a lap for fun can never cost a title. The player's own place is booked AT THE LINE (`recordFinish`) so pressing straight on to the next stage cannot lose the stage just won, and the field's places fill in behind it when the stragglers are home (`recordResult`). A save from the version that kept a separate `scandi-flick-championship` season is folded into the one record on read, and the old key is retired on the next write. `results-sheet.tsx` draws the stage's sheet ON the results card (`hud-finish.tsx`): every crew in finishing order with a picture of their car, their time, what the stage paid them and what they have for the location, paged eight to a page — fewer where a phone held sideways leaves less room (`results-pages.ts` cuts the page to the height the card has) — opened on the page with the player's own row lit, and provisional from the moment the card comes up, with the crews still out marked OUT at the bottom until they are home. The pictures are the real bodies in the field's paint, shot once each onto a stamp-sized canvas (`car-portrait.ts`) the moment the field is entered, one per idle slot under the establishing shot (`car-portraits.ts` keeps the roll), so the card never builds a car while it is being looked at. `results-table.tsx` is that same board asked what SIX stages added up to: the location's table, behind the STANDINGS press at the top of a location page — the same rows at the same pitch with the same pictures off the same roll, a stage's TIME swapped for the WINS that break a tie on points, paged the same way and opened on the player's own row. It orders its pictures when the location page mounts rather than when the press is made, so the board has them by the time anybody asks.
- `src/game/main-menu.tsx` + `menu-levels.tsx` + `menu-headsup.tsx` + `menu-car.tsx` + `car-stats.ts` + `menu-glyphs.tsx` — the menu's pages and the PRE-RACE CARD between them and the road. `menu-levels.tsx` holds the STAGE GRID the three stage pages share — campaign, time trial and heads up show the same boxes behind three different gates, so the gate is passed in and nothing else about a stage box is a page's business; `menu-headsup.tsx` is the third of those pages, with the mode's two settings (rivals, participants) under the grid. All three ask WHICH COUNTRY first and show one location's six boxes after — `LocationList`, in `menu-levels.tsx`, is that step, the same row of banners on all three with only the gate behind it differing. Picking a stage on either grid does not start it: it opens the card, where the car turns on its stand and the transmission is chosen, and START is the only thing that begins a run. A road and a car are two decisions, and the car picker was invisible as a row under six stage boxes. THE CAR IS THE CARD: the stand takes the column that grows with the screen (`car-turntable.ts` frames the shot from the car's own measured size and the shape of the canvas, so a phone's tall pane and a laptop's wide one both come back full of car), its name stands across the head of the frame and its one line of billing across the foot, and everything else is six short readings and the transmission beside it. The sheet itself is DERIVED — `car-stats.ts` reads the catalog (`engine/game/defs/cars.ts`) for every figure and every bar, so a car retuned there bills itself correctly with no second table to update, and the bars are scaled across the ROSTER rather than from zero because what the player is choosing between is three cars, not three absolutes. It bills FOUR axes and no more (acceleration, top speed, grip, drifting): the eight it once carried spent the space the car should have been standing in, and half of them — mass, gears, braking, turn-in — were facts nobody chooses a car on. The two the transmission moves also carry a figure, and those count to their new values (`lib/count.ts`) while the bars slide, so flipping the box is a change the player watches rather than one they have to notice. `menu-glyphs.tsx` is the drawn vocabulary every one of those pages leads with — a trophy for the championship and for a stage's best finish, a stopwatch for the clock and for a lap record, a chequered flag, a road, a camera, a padlock — because a menu that writes a sentence under every row is a menu explaining itself, and six explanations is a card that fills a phone.
- `src/game/scores.ts` + `score-board.tsx` + `hud-initials.tsx` over `initials-entry.ts` — the time trial's high score table: ten rows a stage, up to three letters each, one localStorage key per stage plus one for the name last entered. Deliberately not the same thing as the campaign's `best` time (`campaign.ts`), which is what the HUD's target clock and the ghost read and which has no business knowing who was driving. The entry opens on ONE letter rather than AAA, and answers two controls: typing (a tiny contenteditable keyboard bridge under the card raises a phone's or a handheld's own keyboard when a slot is tapped) writes the letter and leaves the next slot empty, while the wheel — up and down on a pad or the arrow keys, the card taking those directions off `menu-nav` through `data-nav-own` — wakes an empty slot holding the letter to its left, so a name is a few short walks along the alphabet. - `src/game/mirror.ts` — the rear-view mirror: a second pass over the same scene from a camera bolted to the CAR looking back — stood on the cockpit's own mirror inside the cabin (`MirrorMount`, worked out by `car/cockpit.ts` from the spec) and aimed back through the backlight, so what it sees is the road through that window and the film the stage has thrown on it — composited as a strip of glass at the top of the frame. HOW WIDE it looks and how far up the window it is pointed are the CAR's answers rather than the mirror's, fitted to the body's own rear pane by `car/mirror-fit.ts`: the backlight is the aperture the whole picture arrives through, so a lens opened wider than it spends the strip on lining and pillar, and one aimed at the middle of it stares at the ground a metre behind the car. The fit finds the largest frame of the glass's own shape that lands entirely on the window and then tilts it as high in that window as it will go — the adjustment a driver makes — which comes out at 26°–28° across on the catalog's bodies, with the horizon a quarter of the way down the strip. It is solved in the plane the frustum is a rectangle in and against the tilted basis the renderer will actually build, because a frame fitted as an offset in the untilted one is not the frame that gets drawn and its corners are the first thing to slide off the glass. On DETAIL ▸ EFFECTS ▸ FULL the glass is also CURVED (`MIRROR_GLASS`): a resolve pass bends the picture the way a real mirror's bent pane does — the middle of the strip drawn about a third larger than its ends, a horizon that bows rather than ruling straight across, and the glass turning away into its housing at the rim. The curve is pinned at the left and right edges and only ever reaches inward, which is what lets it sit on top of a lens fitted exactly to the window: a curve that reached outward would go looking for road in the pillar beside it. The lens is placed off the drawn car's own sprung chassis (`mirrorFrame` in `car-mesh.ts`) rather than off a second copy of the body's transform chain, so the springs' heave, the loft off a brow, the engine's tremble and a corner riding on its bare hub all carry it identically — a lens that took some of those and not others drifts against the lining around it, which reads as the picture bumping every time the road goes light. The car settles which cabin the lens looks back through for the length of the pass (`mirrorPass` in `car-mesh.ts`): the first-person cabin up, the field's interior down, the glass thinned to what a pane looked through is, and the mirror's own pane out so the pass never samples the texture it is drawing into. It is deliberately independent of the camera mode — bonnet or helicopter, the strip answers the same question — and the image is flipped left-for-right, which is why it goes through a render target rather than a scissored viewport: negating the projection's x would reverse every triangle's winding. It sees a fraction of the forward view's distance with the fog pulled in to match (`environment.withHaze`), so the world leaves its frustum where the air had already gone solid rather than being cut off in mid-view. The bottom edge it works out to is restated in `styles.css`, which is what the co-driver's calls hang under. The picture has TWO homes, which is why the pass is split into a `fill` and a `composite`: from the COCKPIT it is drawn into the mirror hanging in the windscreen (`car/cockpit.ts`) instead of onto the strip, so the rear view swings with the body on its springs and slides across the frame as the driver's head is thrown about — and the fill therefore runs BEFORE the frame rather than after it, because geometry can only sample a texture that already exists. A player option, and the most expensive one: it roughly doubles the driving frame's draw calls before the range is pulled in. Expensive enough that it is also reachable from the road: `src/game/hud-mirror.tsx` puts a switch on the glass itself, so a tap puts the whole pass out and leaves the mirror standing where it hung, flat grey inside its own bezel with REAR VIEW MIRROR OFF floating on it, and a tap on that same grey brings the picture back (it says REAR VIEW MIRROR ON over the returning road and fades, because a mirror with the road in it needs no label). The glass deliberately does NOT move or shrink between the two: the target is the same fifth of the screen wide either way, so switching the rear view off never makes switching it back on a smaller thing to hit — which is the whole difference on a phone. Blanking is the RENDERING only and it is never saved — the HUD option is the mirror's existence, this is what it is showing for one session — and switching that option back on puts the picture back with it. Everything under the mirror reads which of the three states it is in (`GlassSlot`): the blanked glass takes exactly the room the picture did, so what frees the co-driver's slot is a mirror that is not there at all rather than one that is switched off. Which is why HOW OFTEN it is redrawn and HOW FAR it sees are not its own to decide — `src/game/mirror-pace.ts` is a ladder walked by the measured frame rate, and the mirror is the first thing on screen to give way when that rate goes: sixty a second at full reach while the machine is holding sixty fps, thirty when it is not, and fifteen at half the reach when thirty was not enough. The rate is the cheap rung — the strip keeps compositing the last answer in between, so what halves is the cost and not the picture — and the reach is the one that pays twice, because the mirror's frustum is also what forbids the world cull from throwing away the country behind the car. Falling is cheap (a second under the target) and climbing is a probe that gets more expensive every time it has to be given back, so a machine that cannot hold the faster pace stops being asked. `mirror_test.ts` owns the rules; the debug overlay's CAMERA box prints the rung in force under the fps.

- `src/game/camera.ts` — camera **modes**. Nine can be driven from and are one ladder walked from the nose backwards (`bumper`, `hood`, `cockpit`, `close`, `chase`, `far`, `heli`, `top`, `tv`); `drone` and `map` are placed by the app for the menu backdrop and the Roam page, each in a rig of its own (`camera-drone.ts`, `camera-map.ts`), and what an outside rig may stand on — the footprint the ground is read over, the play it hangs on and the floor it may never sink under — is `camera-ground.ts`. Walking the ladder is a MOVE and never a cut (`camera-change.ts`). The five outside ones are the same rig with different proportions — one table of numbers, one update function — so an angle is a row rather than another camera. They track a blend of nose and travel direction so drift angle reads on screen. What makes `close`, `chase` and `far` one shot at three lengths is the LOOK-OVER ANGLE — the depression from the lens to the top of the car's own roof, about 13° for `chase` and `far` — because that angle, not the height, is what decides whether the player can see where they are going: it sets how far up the frame the roofline reaches and how much road is left above it. Height is therefore a consequence of the standoff rather than a free number, and a camera parked at roof height, however short the boom, is a camera looking at a roof. The angle is only the means, though, and where the ROOFLINE lands is the end — the two agree only while the car fills the same amount of frame, and from a four-metre boom it does not. So `close` takes about 18° instead, which is what puts its roofline back where the rest of the ladder puts one; what makes it the tight shot is the length of the boom, not the height of it. `close` and `chase` — the two the game is actually driven from — also carry identical steadying numbers and differ only in where they stand, because a longer boom that also answers more slowly turns the same yaw lag into more metres of world sloshing across the frame; the weight that makes a camera read as FLOWN starts at `far`, whose lateral swing is a sprung mass that overshoots a turn and settles back. The ground they may never sink under is read over the camera's own footprint and only ever sinks at a bounded rate, so a step in the terrain is flown down rather than cut to — with one exception, a car in free fall, which may outrun that ceiling and takes the floor down with it rather than being left below a lens still standing on the lip. OFF THE GROUND the boom stops being a horizontal rod: it turns to lie along the car's own flight path (`flight` in `camera-feel.ts`), dipping under a car coming off a lip so the shot looks up the arc against the sky and swinging over a falling one so it looks down the fall at the ground coming up, with the AIM turning with it so the car sits in the same place in the frame the whole way. Turning the rod rather than moving the lens is what makes it safe at any angle: the rod's LENGTH never changes, so the car is exactly as big in the frame a hundred metres down an alpine face as it was on the road at the top, and the camera never lets it fall away. The angle is a share of the path angle, capped well short of a right angle (a rod stood straight up over a car falling straight down leaves `lookAt` nothing to build a frame from), asymmetric — a nod under a climbing car, most of the way over a falling one — and carried on an under-damped spring, so a designed jump only gets partway to the angle it asks for while a mountainside gets all of it, and the wheels coming down swing the rod back THROUGH level and settle it, which is the bounce at the end of the gesture. `make aircam` is the sheet for all of it. They hang off the car's height on a little PLAY rather than on the height itself, because the road has a cross-section (R16) and the car rides it: absorbing a wheel track, the crown and the step onto the verge is what stops a corner heaving the whole picture on a stage that is dead flat, while anything bigger than the play — a crest, a landing, a cliff — still moves the shot one for one. What a BLOW does to them is `camera-shake.ts`, and the rule there is that a boom did not hit the tree: a blow is classed by what caused it, and an outside rig takes NONE of a contact — running into a boulder, clipping a kerb block, being shunted by another car — because the engine has already dropped the body onto its springs, dipped the nose and rolled the shell, and that car rocking in a steady frame IS the hit. What it does take, from the things that happen to the whole car at once (a landing, a ford, a respawn), is a decaying WAVE of a couple of centimetres rather than a fresh random offset every frame, capped at a frequency the slowest frame rate the game is played at can still draw as a wave — with per-rig scaling, because distance is its own damping. `map` frames a whole stage from the sky and takes the Roam page's drags, wheels and pinches (turn, tilt, zoom) plus a modifier-held drag that WALKS it sideways; it also solves its own near and far planes, because a driving near plane under a kilometres-away far one leaves the depth buffer metres of resolution and every lakeshore shimmers. Zoom has no fractional floor — what stops it is a standoff in metres, so leaning in runs down to a few metres off the ground on an eleven-kilometre epic exactly as it does on a sprint — and the aim rides the GROUND under it rather than the world's zero, which is the difference between standing over a hillside and standing inside it. The pan is held inside the framed island, and the frustum is cut from the distance to that island's centre rather than to the aim the pan has walked away from. What it frames is cut to an island (`map-island.ts`), with the route drawn over it (`map-route.ts`) until leaning in makes that annotation wider than the ground it is drawn on, and — for a developer — one of the generator's own layers painted over the lot (`map-layers.ts`). `tv` (`camera-tv.ts`) is the one mode on the ladder whose pose is not hung off the car at all, which is why `RIDING_MODES` exists beside `PLAY_MODES`: it is a GALLERY of fixed tripods planted along the road before the run starts, and the only thing that happens while the car drives is that a director cuts from one to the next. The stands are placed by a walk over the track's samples — one just inside the turn-in of every bend that holds long enough to be a corner, on the OUTSIDE of it (`-sign(curvature)`, the side away from the crowd and into everything the outside rear throws), a second past the exit of a long one, one beside every jump landing, and a plain roadside stand wherever a stretch has earned nothing for `maxGap` metres, which is what keeps a long straight — or the training ground, which is one — from leaving the director with a single camera and a car driving into the distance. Each stand is set back beyond the road's own edge, stood on the ground read over a footprint (`camera-ground.ts`) and floored against the road's crown so a tripod on the low side of a shelf is not filming a bank. The cut is LATE — the director holds a stand until the car is `hold` metres past it, so every shot keeps the car for a beat as it leaves — the pan lags the car so the car leads the frame going by, and the lens solves itself from the range so the car fills about the same amount of frame whether it is arriving from two hundred metres or going past at six. `camera_tv_test.ts` holds the placement rules, none of which a screenshot can show. The LENS is half of it: the fov is solved every frame from the range so the car fills about the same width of frame arriving from two hundred metres as it does going past at six, which on a long lens is a real zoom and is most of what makes the shot read as broadcast — and it is eased slower than the car closes, so it is still long as the car arrives and still wide for a beat after it has gone, which is the operator catching up. `camera-tv-lens.ts` is the other half and the only post-process pass in the game that is not the mirror's: DEPTH OF FIELD, solved per pixel from the depth buffer against the range the zoom already used, so the focal plane is where the operator pointed rather than somewhere worked out twice. Every other camera sits a few metres off the car with everything worth looking at at one distance, where a pinhole lens is not a lie; a tripod on a long lens has a focal plane you can SEE. The gather weighs each tap by its own circle of confusion, which is what stops the sharp car bleeding a halo into the soft country behind it. It rides the EFFECTS budget (`TV_BOKEH`, FULL only) and is stood up on the first frame the camera is taken and given back the moment it is not, so no other view pays for it — target included.
- `src/game/camera-feel.ts` — what the outside camera CONVEYS of the car, over its framing: the frame as an instrument. Three readings are folded into the picture, each a knob in `CAMERA_FEEL` and each small enough that a player not looking for it never notices. GRIP AS HEIGHT: the lens stands at its rig's own height while the car is on its whole weight on gravel and hovers up as the grip goes — over a brow, through a landing's skitter, on sand, and to the top of its travel (`hover`, per rig) when there is no grip at all, which is flying; where the tires hold harder than that it settles a little below. ATTITUDE AS TILT: a share of the road's pitch, back up a climb and forward down a drop, the body's dive and squat, and a bank INTO a turn — a touch for a gripped corner, more for a slide — every angle a degree or two. SURGE AS STANDOFF: the boom is given a share of the car's own longitudinal acceleration, falling behind a car that is pulling away and carrying forward over one that is stopping under it, back to the rig's own length the moment the car is neither — a reading of acceleration, where pace itself is the rig's `distPerSpeed` running the boom out with speed and leaving it out. SPEED AS A TREMOR: past the pace the gears reach on their own the lens starts to tremble, growing to a fall's three hundred, so speed bought from gravity is felt. All of it eased, DOM-free, and read by `tests/camera_feel_test.ts`.
- `src/game/camera-eye.ts` — the three cameras taken from INSIDE the car, as one rig with a head on it: `bumper` down at the nose ahead of every panel, `hood` on the car's own scuttle over its bonnet, `cockpit` behind the wheel — the narrowest lens on the ladder, so the screen fills the frame with a pillar just inside each edge and the glass, and whatever the stage has thrown on it, right up against the eye. What separates them from the chase rigs is that a lens bolted to a bodyshell is a still photograph — the stage's ground is a smooth loft, so on a straight it does not move at all. Two things put the motion back. There is a HEAD ON A NECK: the eye sits on top of a neck hinged at the base of it, pushed off upright by what the driver is being pushed by — thrown toward the nose under the brakes, tipped back under power, leaned out of a corner, dropped into a dip — and it arrives a beat after the car and settles without bouncing. The travel is not a free number but anatomy: `length` and `leanMax` give an arc (22 cm and ten degrees, so under four centimetres of picture) that `soften` approaches and never reaches, so no landing, shunt or long frame can move the view further than a neck reaches. Because the eye SWINGS rather than slides, leaning any direction also lowers it a little, which is what couples the axes the way a body does; the spine's own give is the only travel that is not the arc, and it is small. What pushes the head is read off the car's own RATES (`u`, `w`, `vy`, `yawRate`) and never off where the mount has got to, and that choice is the whole difference between a neck and a rattle. At 120 km/h the mount covers half a metre between frames, so a head that chases it has every quantity dominated by road speed rather than by load: a fraction of a percent of disagreement is centimetres of lean nothing asked for, parked in one direction in proportion to SPEED, leaving the brakes nothing left to move — and since the engine steps at a fixed rate off an accumulator, on a display whose rate that does not divide (144 against 60, say) a chased mount alternates between double speed and a dead stop and hands the neck metres a second of motion the car never made, so the machine it was tuned on is steady while somebody else's shakes itself apart. Differencing a VELOCITY has no such term: the error is bounded by the acceleration. The load is then smoothed by a critically damped follower before the neck answers it, and that smoothing IS the head's inertia — everything faster is carried rigidly instead, which moves the cabin and the road together (motion) rather than one against the other (shake). Two poles rather than one, because a single ease steep enough to reject a rough stage is also slow enough to make the brakes arrive late. And the ROAD HAS GRAIN: a three-oscillator vibration applied as MOTION rather than as load, because a mass on a spring at ~3 Hz answers a 10 Hz road with almost nothing — and it is the one thing here nothing filters, which is why the seat with bodywork closest to the lens takes the LEAST of it rather than the most. Its top oscillator is capped by the SLOWEST frame rate the game is played at rather than by how fine a buzz would be nice — past what a frame can draw as a wave it stops describing the road and starts describing the sampling — and the neck saturates into the end of its travel instead of being clamped to it, so a landing never stops the head dead inside one frame. A HIT throws the head along the direction the blow came from and rings a small rotational wobble on top, which from inside the car is the only thing in frame that says anything was struck — and the only thing a hit does to these three, because a lens with a dashboard under it that is offset bodily is the cabin teleporting rather than a car being hit. It is also why these are the three that take a contact at all: behind the wheel there is a head, and the head keeping going is a description of the hit; on a boom there is nothing to keep going. The body's roll reaches the horizon through the same PLAY the chase rigs stand on, separated by TIME rather than size: a bank is held for a corner and a wheel track is crossed in a second. Four of its numbers are knobs (`ViewSettings`, swept from the URL by the tooling): seat height, seat reach, field of view, and how much head motion there is at all — the last is the one the player sees, as OPTIONS' HEAD MOTION switch, and it winds the neck, the grain, the jolt and the wobble down together, to nothing.

- `src/game/camera-change.ts` — the camera GOING from one view to the next. Four steps of the ladder cross between a seat inside the car and a boom behind it, and a cut is the one edit that tells the player nothing there: both frames are of the same car a couple of metres apart, so all a cut says is that the picture has been replaced. So the lens is flown along the straight line between the two poses, turning as it travels, over about a third of a second — climbing in, and stepping back out. The steps that walk ALONG the boom are flown too: the standoff and the height already ease between those rigs, but the AIM is read straight off the rig's row and would otherwise swing a couple of degrees in one frame. The pose the move starts from is held in the CAR's own axes and rebuilt around it every frame, because at 140 km/h the car covers twelve metres inside the move and a path drawn between two world points strands the lens in a field; the destination is re-read every frame off the pose the new rig has already written, so the last flown frame IS the rig's own frame and there is nothing to catch up at the end. That is also why a rig arriving from off the boom is STOOD around the car rather than eased onto it — a destination still travelling is one the move can only chase. A floor under the path, faded in with the length of the move and out again before it lands, keeps the line between a bumper and a boom out of the brow the car is cresting.
- `src/game/car/cockpit.ts` — the first-person cabin, built ONCE for the player's car where `car/interior.ts` is built fifteen times for the field: a fascia with a face (louvred vents, the switch panel with a pilot lamp over each rocker, the map light and the fire bottle in the co-driver's footwell), a full-size wheel on a column with its two stalks, pedals and a dead pedal, cage bars up the screen pillars, door pulls and winders, the intercom and the extinguisher pull on the tunnel, a sun strip and a rear-view mirror hanging in the top of the windscreen with the mirror pass's own picture in it. Every reading comes off `car-instruments.ts`, which the HUD's snapshot reads too, so the dash and the cluster can never disagree — and from the seat the HUD's cluster stands down, because the dash IS the cluster. It only fits because the body's top deck is CUT AWAY under the cabin for a car that will be sat in (`OpenCabin` in `car/shell.ts`): the loft is a closed body, so out of the box the cabin is a tray 350 mm deep between that deck and the roof, and a seated driver and a 320 mm wheel do not fit in 350 mm. Cutting it makes the room a metre deep and makes this file responsible for closing it again — a floor at footwell height, an inner sill each side and a bulkhead at each end — while `car/interior.ts`'s own pan closes it in every view the cockpit is not up in. Three materials, and each split buys something: the cabin is darker than the paint outside it and goes to almost nothing at night, the instruments — the dials, the readouts and the lamps — answer to nothing the sky does, and the sun strip is translucent.
- `src/game/car/screen-rain.ts` — the water on that windscreen, from the driver's seat and nowhere else. `car/wipers.ts` carries the road grime on the VERTICES of a tessellated pane, which is the right machine for a slowly deepening wash seen from any camera on the ladder; water is the opposite problem — thousands of discrete beads a millimetre or two across, the few that grow to half a centimetre and break loose, and the threads they leave behind them, and a vertex grid fine enough to carry one would be a million triangles on one window. So the pane here is nine hundred triangles of nothing and every drop on it is solved per pixel, every size in metres of real glass held a shade over life: runners whose heads are the biggest water on the screen and still under a centimetre, each leaving a thin continuous rivulet down a lane it has cleared through the beading; three layers of beading at scales that do not divide one another, the finest of which is the carpet of specks a wet screen actually is; and a continuous rippling film under all of it — without which a screen reads as a clean pane with spots painted on it. Three things make it water rather than spots. It REFRACTS: the finished frame is copied to a texture and every covered pixel samples that copy at an offset taken from the drop's own surface slope, scaled by how big that drop actually is on screen over a floor a bead three pixels across still turns the world over inside, so a bead shows the world behind it turned over rather than merely smudged — which is the whole reason this is a second pass rather than another mesh in the scene. It RUNS THE RIGHT WAY: water on a windscreen does not fall — below about 15 km/h it creeps down under its own weight, above it the air over the scuttle drags it UP the screen and a corner leans the runs toward the outside of the bend, and one signed rate covers all of it, crossing zero through a moment where the water hangs almost still. And THE ARM ACTUALLY CLEARS IT: there is no per-pixel memory to write a wipe into, so the glass is asked how long ago the blade went past and works it out from the stroke — the angle is `park + sweep(1 − cos φ)/2`, so the phase at which the blade crosses any point is an arccos with two answers a cycle, and the shortest way back to either is the age of the water there. Everything else is a function of that age: nothing behind the blade, beads swelling over the second after it, a full streaming screen out at the corners the arm never reaches, the faint arcs of smear rubber leaves behind it, and the bow wave standing in front of it. The DOORS carry the same shader on a second mesh, with the frame the runs are solved in turned a quarter: a side window is edge-on to the air, so standing still it only beads, and at speed every drop on it is blown back along the car in a horizontal streak — no gravity term, no crossover, no wiper, and one mesh for all four flank windows. It is the player's car only, only from the seat, and only on an FX budget that can carry a full-frame copy (`GLASS_RAIN` in `settings.ts`: LOW detail wets the glass and does not draw the rain): `WipeState` (`car/wipers.ts`) is the arm's live position, and `car/pane-frame.ts` the change of basis both of them solve in.
- `src/game/gpu-context.ts` — the one part of the render stack the page does not own. A phone browser reclaims the WebGL context from a tab it has put in the background, and rotating the screen in another app and coming back is the everyday way to lose one. Nothing draws in between and three's `render` quietly turns into a no-op, so the canvas — which is transparent — leaves the page's own brand sky filling the screen with the HUD floating on it, and the seconds after the restore are three re-uploading a stage's worth of geometry and recompiling every shader. The watch refuses the loss (which is the only thing that makes it recoverable), tells the renderer to stop drawing and to re-cut the buffer on the way back — the box may have changed while the page was away, and a rotation in the background announces itself to nobody — and tells the app, which puts a live run on the pause card and raises the same LOADING card a race is stood up behind (`loading-screen.tsx`) until there is a picture again. DOM-free and structurally typed like `thumb-guard.ts`, so its rules are checked by the root suite.

- `src/game/input.ts` / `gamepad.ts` / `hud.tsx` / `hud-touch.tsx` — keyboard, touch and controller, and the arcade HUD. A pad fires no events, so `input.ts` polls every connected one once a frame and `gamepad.ts` — DOM-free, reading plain snapshots so the tests can hold a pad still — turns the sticks, the analogue triggers and the buttons into the same axis, pedals and presses the other two surfaces produce. The thumb zones themselves are `hud-touch.tsx`, split off the HUD because they are the one surface that does not run off the snapshot — they write into the input manager at pointer rate — over three DOM-free policy modules the tests can read: `thumb-guard.ts` (every way a grip on a finger has to be able to END), `pedal-gesture.ts` (what a drag MEANS, and the timing that separates a gear flick from the brake bound to the same direction) and `shift-window.ts` (which gears a flick may take: a key or a pad shoulder is a deliberate press and shifts whenever asked, a thumb is held to gears the revs will hold). A connected pad takes the thumb zones off the screen, and `menu-nav.ts` (over the DOM-free `menu-cursor.ts`) lets it walk the menus — generically, off the buttons already on screen, so a page added tomorrow is navigable the day it is written. Four things are authored on top of that geometry, as data attributes a page carries: `data-nav-back` (what B presses), `data-nav-next` (what START presses — the screen's own way ON, which is what makes the front door → the ladder → the car → the green light one button held down), `data-nav-focus` (where the cursor stands when the surface comes up, defaulting to the way on) and `data-nav-steps` (an arrow either side of a value — the car on its stand, Roam's seed — walked as ONE stop whose left and right move the value rather than the cursor; a range input is the same idea with no arrows drawn on it). Together that is what makes an installed PWA on an Android handheld playable end to end with the controls it already has. The top-right map is three modules and the split is the design. `minimap-scene.ts` cuts the COUNTRY — a fixed square of world seen from above, centred on the car and travelling with it wherever it goes, drawn as a cartoon of the game rather than a picture of the stage: the road as a fat ribbon with its sealed runs picked out, every other road beside it (branches, public roads, drives, the railway kept dashed so nobody reads it as a turning), the standing water and the streams, the OPENINGS in the forest a car could get through, and the buildings as footprints. Nothing in it is to scale but the ground, because a nine-metre road at this framing is three pixels. Two things keep it affordable: the paths are cut around an ANCHOR and then translated to the car, so the picture scrolls every snapshot while the geometry behind it is rebuilt a couple of times a second (about a millisecond when it is); and the window's SPAN — which opens up with the speedo, so the frame holds a roughly constant amount of warning rather than a constant piece of road, and opens further again on a heads-up race, where the map is also carrying the field — is quantised for the cut and the remainder carried as a scale on the group, so the zoom is continuous and free. `minimap-view.ts` places everything that MOVES over it, and each mark answers being off the window differently: the car cannot leave, since the window is centred on it; a rival simply goes; and the split board the run still owes (R28) never goes — a ring while it is on the map, a chevron on the rim pointing at it while it is not, which is the mark to steer at from a field or a wrong turn, and the one mark that can be BEHIND the car, because a board missed is a board still owed. On a heads-up race, where everybody left on one green and the order of the road is a real order, the field is drawn as numbered plates in each crew's own paint, painted backmarker first so the leader is the plate nothing can cover and the player's own car goes on after all of them. `minimap.tsx` is the DOM and the glyphs, and the gauge on the frame's own border; tapping it opens the in-race pause card (`menu.tsx`, which also holds the option vocabulary every menu surface shares). Directly under it stands the CONDITION SCHEMATIC (`hud-health.tsx`, off the DOM-free fold in `car-health.ts`): a plan of the player's own car with a colour per piece — green well, yellow damaged, orange very damaged, red broken — over a row of marks for the machinery that has no shape worth drawing from above. It is the standing half of a damage model whose other half is SAID: a call in the middle of the screen is news and ages out, and this is the answer to what is left. The two can never disagree, because the four tiers are the ledger's own `callAt` lines and everything that is not a system ledger (a wheel, a crush depth) is remapped into that space before it is coloured. It is judged in `make health` rather than in the game, because seeing it amber from the driver's seat means crashing the car amber first. The HUD re-renders from a ~12 Hz snapshot (`snapshot.ts`), which is fine for every instrument except two: the race clock, whose hundredths digit is the point of it, and the start lights, which have to change on the same frame as the tick that goes with them. Those two live in `hud-clock.tsx` and read a `LiveRun` the frame loop rewrites in place — the clock writes its digits straight into the DOM, so sixty updates a second cost two text nodes rather than a re-render of every dial.
- `src/lib/synth.ts` / `src/lib/tracker.ts` / `src/game/audio/` — the sound. A small WebAudio synth (the only module in the tree that touches an `AudioContext`), a tracker sequencer, three sound banks as data (the car and the stage, the world, the interface), the event router, a listener table that moves the whole mix with the camera, and the continuous beds — the engine, the road, the weather and the country's own ambience — built once as steered node graphs rather than fired as grains, so a stalled frame leaves them holding rather than leaving a hole. Seven scores, picked by the stage's country, sky and shape. Nothing is a file: every effect and every note is synthesized from parameters — see [audio.md](audio.md), and hear it with `make audition`.
- `src/game/rumble.ts` + `haptics.ts` — what the player feels in their hands, built the same way the sound is and for the same reason: `rumble.ts` is the whole opinion as one table (a contact sized by closing speed, a slam, a rollover, the crash that ends a run, the gearbox, and the drift as a paced train of ticks read off `car.slide` every frame rather than off an event), DOM-free and testable; `haptics.ts` is the only module that makes a device buzz. A device has ONE motor and no mixer — asking for a pulse while one is running cuts the first one off, not layers it — so the ledger inside `createRunRumble` plays one pulse per step, the biggest thing in it, and refuses anything weaker than what is still running. Two roads out: the Vibration API where a browser has a motor, and the shell seam (`SHELL_RUMBLE`) where an iOS WebView has none. The player's switch is `Settings.rumble`, offered only where `canRumble()` says there is something to switch.
- `src/game/screenshots.ts` + `shot-plan.ts` + `shot-hud.ts` + `menu-gallery.tsx` — the camera the player carries. ENTER (or a pad button; a phone uses its own screenshot key, which is why the top bar carries no shutter) leaves a REQUEST behind and the frame loop serves it: the renderer runs with no `preserveDrawingBuffer`, so the pixels have to be lifted off the drawing buffer inside the same animation callback that drew them, and everything that can wait — the mark stamped into the corner, the PNG encode, the write into the roll — happens after. The picture is the whole SCREEN — the rendered frame with the HUD over it. The instruments are DOM rather than pixels the renderer put down, so they are rasterized in on the way past (`shot-hud.ts`): every piece of chrome the app-root stands over the canvas — the HUD, and the cards above it — is serialized at the SHUTTER (the clock, the call and the place a picture has to carry are the ones that were on screen when the button went down), wrapped with the page's own stylesheet in a `<foreignObject>` so the browser lays out and paints its own HUD rather than a second one being drawn in Canvas2D, and composited under the caption and the mark. A rasterized document HAS NO CLOCK — an SVG image is painted at time zero — so the layer is STILLED (`LAYER_STILL_CSS`) and whatever each animation or transition was part-way through is read off the live element and inlined on the copy. Without that every entrance replays from its first keyframe, and the news column, the split board and the touch wheel all begin at `opacity: 0`: on screen and not in the picture. None of it is load-bearing — a browser that will not paint one gives back the frame it always did — and a frame on its own is still one key away, because ALT takes the chrome off and the HUD option takes it off for good. The one thing that moves to make room is the mark: `stampLift` reads a coarse map of where the instruments actually landed and slides the badge up the corner until it is clear, which on a window held sideways is no distance at all and on a phone held upright is over the cluster along the foot. The DEBUG boxes are drawn rather than captured, and go over the instruments: with the overlay up (or from the developer map's own shutter) the panels and the REPRO line are drawn into the pixels in Canvas2D, because a picture whose whole purpose is to be handed to somebody else has to keep saying where it was taken after it has left the game. What is DECIDED about a picture (its size, its name, where the mark goes) is split into `shot-plan.ts`, which is DOM-free so the tests can hold it to a promise; the mark itself is read from the same `icons/icon.svg` the raster icons are generated from, inlined at build time so an offline picture is signed like any other. The roll is `lib/shot-store.ts` — IndexedDB, because a PNG is not a few kilobytes of JSON and localStorage's whole budget is 5 MB — over the pure policy in `lib/shot-roll.ts` (newest first, capped at forty, oldest out). It is never load-bearing: a private-mode tab with no IndexedDB at all keeps the roll in memory and loses nothing but the pictures at the end of the session. `menu-gallery.tsx` is the viewer — one shot fills the page, the roll is a filmstrip under it — and `lib/share-image.ts` answers the only question the buttons have, which is what THIS browser can do with a PNG: the platform's share sheet, the clipboard, or a download, each offered only where a real probe says it will work. The shutter reaches the same clipboard on its own — `beginImageCopy` claims it INSIDE the press, handing `ClipboardItem` a promise of a picture that will not exist for another few frames, because the encode outlives the gesture's transient activation and a write issued after it is one Safari refuses. Two switches in the settings blob (`screenshots`, `copyShots`), both on by default and neither offered on the options page: whether the shutter takes pictures at all, and whether each one also goes on the clipboard.
- `src/game/race-loader.ts` + `src/game/loading-screen.tsx` — **the load a race is stood up behind.** Standing a stage up is the most expensive thing the game does and almost none of it is the road: the generator compiles the route, the renderer builds a country and a forest, fourteen crews each get a game of their own, and on a field of ghosts every one of them DRIVES THE WHOLE STAGE before the player's lights run (`engine/sim/field.ts`). It used to be paid for twice over and both ways showed — one synchronous call on the press of START, and then whatever was left bleeding into the racing four milliseconds a frame. It is now a list of STEPS, each asked to stop when the frame is spent, driven from the frame loop with a card over the canvas. The sequencing module never learns what a step does, which is what keeps it DOM-free and tested; the steps are closures in `App.tsx`. The frame budget is a SHARE of however long frames are actually taking rather than a fixed number of milliseconds — a fixed one is most of a frame at 60 Hz and one percent of one at 1 Hz, so the slower the device the less of it the load would get. Nothing is deferred past the green: when the card lifts the road is compiled, the world built, the shaders compiled (`renderer.warm`), the crews entered and their whole runs written down. The card says WHICH PHASE is being paid for and where it sits (`Timing the opposition… (4/5)`): a phase is a run of neighbouring steps sharing a label, so the steps stay as fine as the work needs while the count stays as coarse as a person reads, and the spend stops at each boundary so every phase gets a frame to be drawn in before the work behind it starts. Under the line is a bar for that phase alone — filled from a MEASURED fraction where the work can count itself (crews out of an entry list, road written out of a stage: `fieldWritten`), from what the same phase cost on this machine last time where it cannot (`load-times.ts`, since compiling a road is nought and then one), and sweeping on a machine that has neither. All three are transforms, for the same reason the mark is. The press on START makes no sound of its own, and no theme changes while the card is up: the score the player pressed start under is booked far enough ahead to survive the blocked frames and hands over at the lights (`docs/audio.md`).
- `src/game/app-mark.ts` + `mark-tracks.tsx` — the app mark's two SKID MARKS, as something the page can draw. `app-mark.ts` is the geometry as data; `mark-tracks.tsx` fills them from the tail at the bottom to the head at the top — once and left standing beside the menu's wordmark, over and over on the loading card. Only the tracks: the car at the head of them belongs to the ICON and stays there. It fills them with a BAND that slides up over the finished shape rather than with `stroke-dashoffset`, and that is the whole design rather than a detail: a dash offset animates on the MAIN THREAD, which is exactly the thread the loading card is covering for, so on a long stage the card froze solid for the seconds the generator and the world builder each hold it — a hung game rather than a loading one. A transform runs on the compositor and keeps climbing through those blocks (the double translate — window up, drawing down by the same amount — is how a wipe is done with transforms alone). One band covers both tracks, so the pair fills together as one car's wheels would lay it, and the band is faithful to the shape only because both tracks descend STRICTLY, which `tests/app_mark_test.ts` walks them to prove. That suite also holds the data module to `pwa/public/icons/icon.svg`, since the geometry is stated three times and cannot be imported across the three (a static asset, a Node script, a TS module).
- `src/App.tsx` — the fixed-timestep loop (engine at `TUNING.physicsHz` regardless of frame rate, with the drawn frames capped at `FRAME_HZ` on a phone), the daily-seed stage rotation, car swap, and the new-build button (`game/update-button.tsx`, fed by `lib/pwa-update.ts`).
- `pwa-plugin.ts` — hand-rolled service worker + manifest emission per deploy slot (the same pattern as the sibling contacts app); `src/app-pwa.ts` holds the cache-id contract both sides share.
- `src/identity.ts` — the single source for name, copy, palette, and URLs.
- `src/output-bridge.ts` — routes the engine's output module into the app's debug log.
- `src/lib/pwa-update.ts` — the update watch: registers the emitted worker, reports whether a newer build is waiting and which one.

The app has no runtime dependency beyond Preact and three.js; the runtime is Preact throughout, with `react` aliased to `preact/compat` by the Vite preset and `pwa/tsconfig.json`'s `paths`.

## `tests/` and `scripts/`

Root-level vitest suites cover the generator's R-rules, the drift and jump moment by moment, the gearboxes, and full bot simulations (see [simulation.md](simulation.md)). `scripts/` holds Node tooling: the sim CLI, track previews, the audio audition page, headless screenshots, the icon/OG generator (pure-Node PNG encoder in `scripts/lib/png.mjs` — no native image deps), the SEO checker, and the release plumbing (`scripts/release/`, changeset fragments → CHANGELOG).

Several of those tools are the same shape and worth knowing as one: a
harness PAGE under `pwa/src/tools/` with its own HTML entry at the `pwa/`
root, and a `scripts/*.mjs` that builds just that entry, serves it, drives
it in headless Chromium and writes a sheet to `previews/`. `make cars`,
`make sky` and `make items` are all built this way, so the picture that
comes back is drawn by the modules the game ships rather than by the tool.
`make items` is the odd one in what it photographs: a single OBJECT — a
stone, a plant, a kerb post, the cabin behind a car's glass — on a fitted
turntable over a metre grid, which is the only place most of the world's
vocabulary is ever legible.

## `native/` — the store shell

The App Store / Play Store wrapper: a thin Expo / React Native app whose entire content is a full-screen WebView. It is **not** a second client — the built site is packed into the app (`assets/webroot.zip`, by `scripts/bundle-web.mjs`) and served on launch by a local HTTP server on a fixed port (`src/local-server.ts`), so the same bundle the web serves plays offline on the phone with one `localStorage` origin across launches. Three things cross the seam and nothing else: the shell writes `"native"` into the frozen `__SF_SHELL__` global before the page boots (`src/injected.ts` — the same global the desktop app writes `"tauri"` into, read by `pwa/src/shell-host.ts`) so the PWA update lifecycle stays off; an off-site link is handed to the system browser instead of replacing the game (`src/navigation.ts`); and a VIBRATION the page asked for is relayed to the phone's haptics (`src/rumble.ts` decides, `src/haptics.ts` plays), because a WKWebView has no Vibration API and the website's own rumble table (`pwa/src/game/rumble.ts`) would otherwise go unfelt on iOS. Those decision modules are pure, and the root suite tests all three. It has its own dependency tree outside the npm workspace, so `make lint` and `make test` stop at its edge; `make native-typecheck` covers it. **The app depends on the site; nothing in `engine/` or `pwa/` may learn the shell exists** beyond that one word in the shared global — a bridge is the page describing what it wants and the shell spending it, never a feature that lives in the shell. Adding a platform service later is another bridge module under `native/src/` and a flag on the WebView message channel. → [`native/README.md`](../native/README.md), [platforms.md](platforms.md).

## Deployment

The app deploys to GitHub Pages at [game2.niclaslindstedt.se](https://game2.niclaslindstedt.se/) in three slots — `/` (latest release tag), `/preview/` (main), `/branch/` (parked feature branch) — via `pages.yml`; `release.yml` cuts versions from changeset fragments, packages the desktop downloads and chains the deploy. ## `tauri/` — the desktop app

The same built site, bundled inside a native window for Windows, macOS and Linux: a thin [Tauri](https://tauri.app) shell in the platform's own webview, serving `pwa/dist` from a private `game://` scheme so the player's origin-keyed storage survives every update. Two Rust crates — `shell/` is every decision and has no GUI dependency (its whole suite runs anywhere a Rust toolchain does), `src-tauri/` is every effect — with their own toolchain and their own checks (`make tauri-test`, `make tauri-lint`), outside the root suite's path. The engine never learns it exists; the app's one read of it is `pwa/src/shell-host.ts`, which keeps the PWA update lifecycle off inside the window. [platforms.md](platforms.md) says what it adds and what it deliberately does not; [`tauri/README.md`](../tauri/README.md) is the tree.
